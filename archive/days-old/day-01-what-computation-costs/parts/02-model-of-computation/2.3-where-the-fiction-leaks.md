# 2.3 — Where the fiction leaks

## 1. The one-line answer

The imaginary machine is wrong in three specific ways you can measure on your own laptop before
the day is out, and knowing which of the three is biting you is the difference between an
optimisation that works and one that just makes the code uglier.

---

## 2. The story

By half past eight on a Sunday there are eleven slips clipped to the wire above Kamala's head and
the queue has reached the door of the tiffin room.

She has run this counter for twenty-two years and she has never once hurried. Her nephew Arun,
who has been helping since March, moves twice as fast as she does and produces about half as
much, and neither of them can quite explain why until you watch their feet.

Everything Kamala uses is inside the arc her arm makes without her shoulders moving. Batter in the
steel bucket at her left knee. Chutney and sambar in the two vessels sunk into the counter at her
right hand. Oil in a squat tin by the tawa. Podi in a katori. Ghee in a smaller one behind it.
Before the counter opened she spent a long while filling all of them and setting them down in the
places her hands already expect, and now she does not look at any of them. She reaches.

Arun keeps everything in the back store, where it is tidy. When he needs chutney he walks to the
back, ladles chutney, walks back. He is quick on his feet and the walk is not far. But it happens
that a slip needs chutney, and then podi, and then ghee, and he makes the walk three times, and
the tawa is sitting there with three dosas on it going leathery while he is behind a curtain
being efficient.

Then there is the ghee roast, which is one line on a slip like every other line, and which takes
as long as three plain dosas because of what it is. A slip with four items on it can take four
units of work or eight, and the wire above Kamala's head cannot tell her which. She has learned
to read the slips as they are clipped up, and when she sees two ghee roasts she does something
about it before the queue finds out.

Her sister, who runs a bigger place on the main road, makes the identical dishes with the
identical recipes and serves noticeably fewer people in a morning. The recipes are not the
difference. Nobody has ever written down what the difference is.

## 3. The idea in plain language

The RAM model of 2.1 says: every step costs one, every memory cell is the same distance away.
Kamala's counter is the audit. Three things it is wrong about, in the order they will cost you
money.

**Leak one: memory is not uniform.** Reaching the bucket at your knee is not the same act as
walking to the back store, and a real processor is built entirely around this fact. There are
small fast memories close to it — the **caches** — and a large slow one further away, and the
gap between them is not ten per cent, it is roughly two orders of magnitude. Worse, memory does
not arrive one cell at a time. It arrives in fixed-size blocks called **cache lines**, sixty-four
bytes on essentially every machine you will use, which means fetching one number drags in its
seven neighbours whether or not you wanted them. A procedure that then *uses* those neighbours
gets them free. A procedure that jumps somewhere else pays again. That is the whole of Arun's
problem: he has been making one trip per item to a place that would have given him seven items
per trip.

**Leak two: an operation is not always one step.** The ghee roast is one line on the slip and
three dosas of work. Python's integers are the same shape of lie: they are not machine words,
they grow to whatever size they need, and multiplying two of them costs more the bigger they get.
2.1's third assumption — one cell, one number, one step — is the assumption Python breaks by
design, and it breaks it silently, because `a * b` looks the same whatever `a` is.

**Leak three: the constant factor is invisible to the theory and decisive in the room.** Kamala
and her sister run the same recipes at different throughput. `Θ` was built to throw away exactly
that difference, and it is right to: constants change when you change machine and the shape does
not. But a factor of thirty-four is still a factor of thirty-four when the deadline is Friday
(1.1 §7 measured one), and an engineer who cannot say which of two `Θ(n log n)` procedures to
ship has a real gap.

The unifying statement is worth having in one sentence, because it turns three complaints into
one bounded claim.

> **Formally:** the model's cost and the real cost differ by a factor no larger than the ratio
> between the most and least expensive thing you charged one step for. Keep that ratio small and
> the model predicts well. Charge one step for both an in-cache read and a page fault, and the
> model may be wrong by a factor of a hundred thousand — while remaining, in the `Θ` sense,
> entirely correct.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **CPython's own list sort.** Open `Objects/listobject.c` and you will find that Timsort does
  not recurse all the way down. Below a threshold — the run length CPython calls `minrun`, kept
  between 32 and 64 — it stops dividing and runs a binary insertion sort instead, a procedure
  that is `Θ(n²)` and that wins anyway at that size because its constant is tiny and it touches
  memory in a straight line. That is leak three and leak one, decided in C, in the standard
  library you call every day. `SRT-11`, Day 51.

- **LMDB, and every B-tree ever shipped.** A balanced binary search tree and a B-tree are both
  `Θ(log n)` lookups, and no database on earth uses the binary one for on-disk data. LMDB stores
  its B+tree in 4 KiB pages, each holding hundreds of keys, so a lookup in a billion keys is a
  handful of page reads instead of thirty pointer-chases to thirty unrelated addresses. Same
  `Θ`. Different base of the logarithm, and — the part that matters — one block transfer per
  level instead of one per comparison. The whole structure is a response to leak one. `SYS-05`,
  Day 213.

- **Asked as:** *"Both of your solutions are `O(n log n)`. Which one would you ship?"* There is
  no complexity-theoretic answer, and the interviewer knows it. They are checking whether you can
  reason about constants without hand-waving: which one allocates, which one walks memory in
  order, which one branches unpredictably, and — the answer that gets you the job — *how you would
  measure to find out*.

## 5. The mechanism

Here is the thing the model draws as one flat row, drawn honestly. Figures are for the machine
these measurements were taken on, an Intel Core i3-1115G4 at 3.0 GHz.

```
        the memory hierarchy the RAM model draws as one flat row

   ┌──────────┐
   │ registers│   a few dozen bytes            ~0.3 ns    <- the model's "cost 1"
   └────┬─────┘
   ┌────┴─────┐
   │ L1 cache │   48 KiB per core              ~1.3 ns    <- also the model's "cost 1"
   └────┬─────┘
   ┌────┴─────┐
   │ L2 cache │   1.25 MiB per core            ~5 ns      <- also the model's "cost 1"
   └────┬─────┘
   ┌────┴─────┐
   │ L3 cache │   6 MiB, shared                ~15 ns     <- also the model's "cost 1"
   └────┬─────┘
   ┌────┴─────┐
   │   DRAM   │   your 8 or 16 GiB             ~80 ns     <- also the model's "cost 1"
   └────┬─────┘
   ┌────┴─────┐
   │   SSD    │   your disk                    ~100000 ns <- also the model's "cost 1"
   └──────────┘

   and memory moves in 64-byte lines, so one fetch brings 8 adjacent 8-byte values
```

*Notice the right-hand column: every row is the model's "cost 1", top to bottom, and the top and
bottom differ by a factor of about three hundred thousand. The model is not approximately right
here. It is right about the count and silent about the price, and the price spans five orders of
magnitude.*

**The invariant:** *the model's answer is the real answer multiplied by something between the
cheapest and the most expensive operation you called "one" — so the model is trustworthy exactly
to the extent that you kept that range narrow.* This is the sentence to keep. It tells you when to
believe a complexity claim (all your operations are arithmetic on small integers in cache) and
when to stop believing it (some of your "operations" go to disk).

### A trace

The experiment that isolates leak one. Take a list of `n` integers, add them all up, and vary
nothing except **the order in which the positions are visited**. Every version does exactly `n`
index reads and `n` additions — identical operation counts, identical model cost, `Θ(n)` for all.

| `n` | footprint of the list | in index order | in shuffled order | ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,024 | ~40 KiB | 0.1166 ms | 0.1257 ms | 1.08 |
| 4,096 | ~160 KiB | 0.4505 ms | 0.4527 ms | 1.00 |
| 16,384 | ~640 KiB | 1.5734 ms | 2.1129 ms | 1.34 |
| 65,536 | ~2.5 MiB | 6.0803 ms | 19.4762 ms | 3.20 |
| 262,144 | ~10 MiB | 27.3589 ms | 148.2801 ms | 5.42 |
| 1,048,576 | ~40 MiB | 109.5547 ms | 651.1056 ms | 5.94 |

Read the ratio column downwards and then read the footprint column beside it. At 160 KiB the two
orders cost the *same* — the list fits in L2, everything is close, the model is telling the truth.
The ratio lifts off at 640 KiB, is at 3.20 by the time the footprint passes the 1.25 MiB of
per-core L2, and jumps again past the 6 MiB of L3. The model did not become wrong gradually
because of something in the code. It became wrong at a *size*, and the size is a number printed
on the processor.

## 6. Line by line

The measurement above, so you can run it rather than believe it.

```python
def sum_in_order(xs: list[int], order: list[int]) -> int:
    total = 0
    for i in order:
        total += xs[i]
    return total
```

One function, used for both arms of the experiment. This matters more than it looks: the two arms
must execute *the same bytecode* so that interpreter overhead, loop mechanics and addition costs
appear identically on both sides and cancel out of the ratio. Writing two similar functions and
comparing them would leave you unable to say whether you had measured memory or measured your own
two functions.

```python
forward = list(range(n))
shuffled = list(range(n))
random.shuffle(shuffled)
```

The order is a separate list, and this is the design decision the experiment rests on. `order` is
itself walked front to back in both arms, so reading `order` costs the same in both. The only
thing that differs between the arms is the *sequence of addresses* handed to `xs`. That is a
controlled experiment: one variable moved, everything else nailed down.

```python
random.seed(20260823)
```

Seeded, because "nothing floats" — an unseeded shuffle gives you a number you cannot ask anyone
else to reproduce, and a benchmark nobody can re-run is an anecdote (Rule 15).

**The near-miss.** Here is the version that seems to test the same thing:

```python
values = [random.randint(0, 10**9) for _ in range(n)]
random.shuffle(values)                    # <- randomise the VALUES
total = sum_in_order(values, forward)     # <- and still walk front to back
```

It reads as "I randomised the data, so the access is random now". It is not. Shuffling the values
permutes what the array *holds*; walking with `forward` still steps through the array's addresses
one after another, in a straight line. What you have randomised is not the thing that costs
money.

Measured, all three arms in the same process at `n = 4,000,000`:

```
ordered walk            :    311.5 ms
shuffled index order    :   2843.0 ms   ratio  9.13x
shuffled values, ordered:   1066.3 ms   ratio  3.42x
```

The near-miss lands at 3.42× rather than at 1.00×, and the honest reading of that is more
interesting than either answer would have been. A CPython list does not hold integers; it holds
*pointers* to integer objects. Walking `forward` reads the pointer array in a straight line —
fast, cache lines fully used — but shuffling the values means those pointers now aim at objects
scattered around the heap, so the second half of each access is still a jump. You get part of the
penalty because you randomised part of the walk. The full 9× needs both halves scattered.

Two lessons, and the second is the one worth keeping. First: know what your language actually
stores, because "a list of integers" in Python is a list of addresses and that changes the memory
behaviour of everything. Second: **when a measurement lands between your two predictions, that is
information and not noise.** The gap is the model telling you it has another term in it.

(One more thing about those numbers, in the spirit of stating what a benchmark hides. The ordered
walk reads 311.5 ms here and 166.4 ms when measured on its own, because this run keeps three
four-million-element lists alive at once and the machine is under memory pressure throughout.
Ratios *within one run* are comparable. Absolute figures across runs are not, and reporting them
as though they were is the most common way a benchmark lies.)

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation.**

Model the memory as two prices instead of one, and count how often each is paid. Let `B` be the
number of values per cache line, `t_hit` the cost of a value already close by, and `t_miss` the
cost of fetching a line from main memory. For this machine: a cache line is 64 bytes, a pointer
is 8 bytes, so `B = 8`; take `t_hit ≈ 1 ns` and `t_miss ≈ 80 ns` from §5.

**Walking in order.** Accesses `0, 1, 2, …, n−1` touch each line once and then use all `B` of its
values. Over `n` accesses the number of line fetches is `n / B`:

```
T_ordered(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) t_hit  +  (n / B) · t_miss
             = n · 1  +  (n / 8) · 80
             = n · (1 + 10) = 11n  nanoseconds
```

**Walking shuffled.** Each access lands on an unrelated line, and the seven neighbours it drags in
are never used before the line is evicted. Every access fetches:

```
T_shuffled(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) (t_hit + t_miss)
              = n · (1 + 80) = 81n  nanoseconds
```

```
predicted ratio = 81n / 11n ≈ 7.4
```

Measured: 9.13× in the three-arm run, 13.30× when the ordered arm is measured alone. The
prediction is the right order of magnitude and it is *low*, and being able to say why it is low is
the actual skill:

- **Two dependent loads per element, not one.** As §6 found, `xs[i]` reads a pointer and then
  reads the object it points at. In the shuffled arm both are misses, and the second cannot begin
  until the first returns, so they do not overlap. Charging `2 · t_miss` gives a predicted ratio
  near 14.6, and the measurement sits between the two predictions — as it should, since some
  objects do land on shared lines.
- **Address translation misses too.** At 40 MiB of footprint, random access defeats the small
  cache of page translations as well, and each miss there costs its own walk through memory. The
  error term has an error term. This is not a defect in the derivation; it is the derivation
  telling you where to look next.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `≈ 11n` ns | Every access adjacent to the last: a straight walk over a contiguous array |
| Average | `≈ 11n` to `81n` ns, by locality | The honest average needs a distribution over *access patterns*, not over data |
| Worst | `≈ 81n` ns, and far worse past RAM | Every access on its own line and its own page — a shuffled walk over a large array |

**Space:** unchanged, `Θ(1)` auxiliary, and this is the point. Not one byte of allocation differs
between the two arms. A thirteen-fold difference in running time with an identical operation count
and an identical memory footprint is as clean a demonstration as exists that the model is counting
something real and pricing it wrong.

**What the constant hides — the other direction.** The interpreter is the reason these ratios are
not larger. Roughly 30 ns per iteration goes on CPython's loop machinery regardless of where the
data lives, and that fixed cost is added to *both* arms, which drags the ratio toward 1. In C the
same experiment separates further. Python's overhead is, in this one narrow sense, a mercy: it
hides leak one until your data is big, at which point it stops hiding it all at once.

## 8. When it breaks

Leak two — an operation that is not one operation — has a failure so sharp that CPython grew an
error message specifically to stop it:

```
>>> int("1" * 5000)
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "<stdin>", line 1, in <module>
ValueError: Exceeds the limit (4300 digits) for integer string conversion: value has 5000 digits; use sys.set_int_max_str_digits() to increase the limit
```

Read that carefully, because it is not an ordinary limit. Converting a decimal string to an
integer is quadratic in the number of digits, so a 100,000-digit numeral submitted to a web form
that calls `int()` on it burns CPU for a long time on one request. That is a denial of service
built out of a function nobody thinks of as a function — and it was assigned CVE-2020-10735 and
fixed in Python 3.11 by refusing to convert more than 4,300 digits unless asked. A language
changed its semantics, in a security release, because "one operation" was not one operation.

The same leak, measured rather than raised. Multiply two `d`-digit integers, doubling `d`:

```
  digits     a*b (us)     ratio
     100        0.305         -
     200        0.842      2.76
     400        3.063      3.64
     800       13.140      4.29
    1600       45.778      3.48
    3200      124.115      2.71
    6400      464.365      3.74
   12800     1347.373      2.90
```

If multiplication were one step, that ratio column would read `1.00` all the way down. It reads
between 2.7 and 4.3. Around 4 is what schoolbook multiplication gives — doubling the digits
quadruples the work, since each of `d` digits meets each of `d` others, `Σ d = d²`. And notice the
column drifting *down* toward 3 in the lower rows: past a threshold, CPython stops using the
schoolbook method and switches to one that trades a multiplication for additions, so doubling the
digits triples the work instead of quadrupling it. That method has a name and a day of its own —
`DNC-03`, Day 89 — and today all you need is the observation that the ratio moved, which means the
procedure underneath your `*` changed while you were not looking.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The fix is not to avoid large integers; they are
often exactly what you need. The fix is to **know which of your operations are unit cost in the
language you are writing in**, and to say so when you state a complexity. `Θ(n)` for a sum of `n`
integers is a claim about small integers. On thousand-digit values the true cost is `Θ(n · w)`
with `w` the words per value, and the claim was never wrong so much as unstated — which is 2.2's
lesson arriving from the other side. Naming the assumption is the whole job; it is also, on this
evidence, a security control.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** At `n = 10⁹` leak one stops being a constant factor you can shrug at and starts
deciding what is possible, and the response in production is always the same: change the *layout*,
not the algorithm.

Google's Abseil `flat_hash_map` is the canonical example. It satisfies the same specification as
`std::unordered_map` and has the same `O(1)` expected lookup, and it is several times faster on
real workloads because it stores entries in one flat array with a small array of control bytes
scanned sixteen at a time with a single SIMD instruction, instead of chasing a pointer per bucket.
Nothing in the complexity changed. The number of cache lines touched per lookup went from several
to about one. Apache Arrow makes the same bet at the level of a whole data format: columns stored
contiguously so that a scan of one field walks a straight line through memory, which is why
analytical engines built on it read a column an order of magnitude faster than a row store with
the identical `Θ`.

The lesson to take into design reviews: **when you have run out of complexity improvements, you
have not run out of performance improvements.** You have arrived at the part the model was never
able to see.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "The complexity is fine, that's not what I'm worried about. You're building a list of pointers
> to a million small objects and then walking it in an order that comes out of a hash map, and
> that's a cache miss per element with nothing to prefetch. Either flatten this into arrays of
> primitives or sort the keys before the walk. And put the measurement in the PR — I don't want
> either of us guessing about a factor of ten."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you have given a correct complexity and a correct constant:

1. *"You've got two `O(n)` passes over an array versus one pass doing both things. Same
   complexity — is there a difference?"* — checking whether you can reason about locality. One
   pass reads the array once and keeps it warm; two passes may read it from memory twice if it
   does not fit. On a small array there is no difference at all, which is the other half of the
   answer.
2. *"When would you use an `O(n²)` algorithm on purpose?"* — checking whether §4's Timsort fact is
   knowledge or trivia. Small `n`, contiguous memory, no allocation, no branch surprises: below a
   threshold the quadratic one wins and the standard library you use every day agrees.
3. *"Your benchmark says the new version is 15% faster. Do you believe it?"* — checking
   measurement discipline. Fifteen per cent is inside the range that machine variance, memory
   layout and process ordering can produce, and the right answer talks about repeats, the minimum
   rather than the mean, and whether the ratio column moved or just the wall clock.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Two procedures perform exactly the same number of operations on the same input, and one is
   thirteen times slower. Name the assumption that has failed.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The second RAM-model assumption: that any memory cell can be reached in one step regardless of
   its address. Memory arrives in 64-byte lines from a hierarchy whose ends differ by a factor of
   roughly a hundred, so the *order* of the accesses prices them, and the model has no way to say
   so.
   </details>

2. Why does the ratio in §5's table start at 1.00 and only lift off later?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because while the whole list fits in cache there is no far away to go to — every access is
   close, whatever order you make them in, so the model is telling the truth. The ratio starts
   moving when the footprint passes a cache size, which is why the interesting column in that
   table is the footprint and not `n`. The same code is well modelled at one size and badly
   modelled at another.
   </details>

3. `a * b` on two Python integers: when is that one operation and when is it not?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   It is effectively one when both fit in a machine word, which for CPython means values below
   about `2⁶³`. Above that, cost grows with the number of digits — near-quadratic in the schoolbook
   range and near `d^1.58` once CPython switches methods, as the ratio column in §8 shows
   happening between 800 and 3,200 digits. The operator looks identical at every size, which is
   what makes this leak dangerous rather than merely present.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** Construct two programs with identical operation counts, identical memory
   footprints and identical `Θ`, whose running times differ by more than 10×. State the *size* at
   which they start to differ.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The §6 experiment is one: sum a list in index order versus in a shuffled order. Identical
   counts, identical footprint, identical `Θ(n)`, measured 13.3× apart at `n = 4,000,000`. They
   start to differ when the footprint passes the last cache that holds it — on the machine in §5,
   between `n = 4,096` (ratio 1.00, ~160 KiB) and `n = 65,536` (ratio 3.20, ~2.5 MiB). The
   sharpest form of the answer names a size, not just a shape, because "cache matters" is a slogan
   and "it lifts off at 640 KiB on this chip" is an engineering statement.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-01`.** 1.2 said "the exponent first, then the constant". Everything in this
   document is about constants. Are the two in conflict?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   No — it is an ordering, not a ranking. Work on the exponent while there is an exponent to fix,
   because no constant survives a doubling of `n` against a better shape. When the shape cannot
   improve, the constant is all that is left and it is worth a factor of ten to thirty, which is
   not a rounding error. What the two rules jointly forbid is the common failure: spending a week
   micro-optimising a quadratic loop that should have been linear, and then shipping it.
   </details>

6. **In review.** A colleague replaces a list of small objects with a list of tuples of primitives
   and reports a 4× speedup with no algorithmic change. What do you ask, and what do you write in
   the comment?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Ask for the size at which they measured and whether the ratio holds as `n` grows — a locality
   win that appears only above a cache boundary is real but conditional, and a reviewer should
   know which side of the boundary production sits on. In the comment, write down *why* it is
   faster (fewer pointer hops, better line utilisation), because a layout optimisation with no
   recorded reason is the first thing a future refactor undoes. Locality changes are invisible in
   the diff and invisible in the complexity; the comment is the only place they exist.
   </details>
