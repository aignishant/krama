# 2.1 — The RAM model, the machine we pretend to have

## 1. The one-line answer

Every claim you will ever make about how long a program takes is a claim about an imaginary
machine on which each simple step costs one and every part of memory is exactly as far away as
every other part.

---

## 2. The story

Sudha runs the quotations desk at a packers-and-movers office off the Nashik road, and she has
never seen the inside of a single flat she has priced.

The phone rings, and a man says he is moving a two-bedroom flat to Pune at the end of the month
and how much. She has him on the line, and there is a competitor two streets over who will answer
this same call if she takes too long over it. So she does what the office has done for nineteen
years: she opens the laminated card taped to the desk, and she counts.

The card is a list of one-line prices. A carton is a carton, and costs the same whether it holds
books or bedsheets. A wardrobe is four cartons. A double bed is six. A fridge is eight. She asks
him five questions, writes six numbers in a column, adds them, multiplies by the distance band,
and reads him a figure. He says he will call back. He calls back.

Everyone in the office knows the card is a lie. Books are heavier than bedsheets. A ground-floor
flat with a loading bay is not the same job as a third-floor one, and a narrow stairwell can cost
a whole extra man for a whole day. The card knows none of this. It has one price per carton and
it applies that price to every carton in Maharashtra.

The card survives, and has survived nineteen years, because of something Sudha's predecessor
understood and wrote on the back of it. The card is not there to predict what a job will cost.
It is there so that two jobs can be *compared* — so that when the Pune man's flat prices at four
hundred units and the Dhule woman's prices at nine hundred, everyone in the office knows the
second job is roughly twice the first, and can staff it accordingly. It is wrong about every job
and right about the ratio between them, and the ratio is what she is being asked for on the
phone.

Twice a year a job arrives that the card cannot price — the fourth floor with no lift, or the
upright piano. Sudha knows those two by name. She has learned to hear them coming.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

The laminated card is a **cost model**. The one that the whole of this curriculum runs on is
called the **RAM model** — Random Access Machine — and it is exactly as flat as Sudha's card.

It makes three assumptions, and it is worth being able to recite them.

**One: each basic operation costs one.** Add, subtract, multiply, compare, assign, read a value
out of memory, write one back. All the same price, all costing one *step*. Adding is not cheaper
than multiplying; reading is not cheaper than writing.

**Two: memory is a numbered row of cells, and any cell can be reached in one step.** This is what
*random access* means, and it is the assumption the model is named after. Cell number 5 and cell
number five billion cost the same to read. There is no near and far.

**Three: one cell holds one number, and that number is small enough to work with in one step.**
"Small enough" has a precise meaning: a cell holds `w` bits, where `w` is at least as large as it
takes to write down an index into the input. Otherwise you could not even hold a position in a
list, and the first assumption would be a fraud — you would be charging one step for arithmetic
your machine cannot actually do in one step.

Given those three, the cost of a program is a *count*: how many steps does this procedure execute
on this input? That count is a whole number. It can be derived on paper, compared between two
procedures, and argued about — which is the entire point, and precisely Sudha's point. The model
is not there to predict milliseconds. It is there so two procedures can be compared, by people
who are not sitting at the same computer, in a way that stays true when the computer is replaced.

Everyone knows the card is a lie. Which parts of it are lies, and how much each lie costs, is
2.3.

> **Formally:** for an algorithm `A` and an input `x`, let `T_A(x)` be the number of unit-cost
> operations `A` executes on `x`. The worst-case cost is `T_A(n) = max { T_A(x) : x is a valid
> input of size n }`. Every complexity claim in this curriculum is a statement about `T_A(n)` on
> this machine, and about no other machine.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **SQLite's query planner.** SQLite has to choose between plans without running them, so it
  carries a cost model in the source: an estimated cost is assembled from per-row and per-page
  constants, and the plan with the smallest estimate is executed. The numbers are hand-tuned and
  the developers document them as approximations. It is Sudha's card, shipped, in a database on
  a couple of billion phones — a flat price list used to rank candidates, not to predict a
  duration.

- **LLVM's instruction cost model.** When the compiler decides whether unrolling a loop or
  vectorising it is worth it, it asks `TargetTransformInfo` what each instruction costs on the
  target chip. And here you can watch the RAM model being *repaired* in production: an integer
  add is priced at 1, but a division is priced far higher, because on real silicon a division is
  genuinely not one add. The model is kept because it is countable, and patched exactly where
  keeping it flat would produce a wrong decision.

- **Asked as:** *"What's the time complexity?"* — asked as though the machine were obvious. It is
  not obvious, it is assumed, and the moment the assumed machine is wrong for the language in the
  room, the expected answer is wrong too. A candidate who says "counting a comparison as one
  step, it's `Θ(n log n)`" has said something an interviewer can check. A candidate who says
  `Θ(n log n)` has said something that is true on a machine nobody named.

## 5. The mechanism

Here is the machine, drawn. There is no picture of it in a textbook because there is nothing to
photograph; this is the whole apparatus.

```
                 the machine every complexity claim is about

    ┌───────────────────┐
    │  control unit     │   executes one step at a time, in order
    │  + a few registers│   each register holds one word
    └─────────┬─────────┘
              │  one cell read or written per step, cost 1
              ▼
  cell    0      1      2      3      4      5     ...    5000000000    ...
        ┌──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬─────┬────────────┬────
  word  │  17  │  -3  │   0  │ 4001 │   9  │  17  │ ... │     42     │ ...
        └──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴─────┴────────────┴────
           ▲                                                  ▲
           └────────── cost 1 ──────────┘                     │
           └──────────────────── also cost 1 ─────────────────┘
```

*Notice the two arrows at the bottom, and that they are the same length. That equality is the
entire content of the phrase "random access", it is the assumption doing the most work in this
model, and it is the one 2.3 will show costing you a factor of thirteen on a machine you own.*

**The invariant:** *every step costs exactly one, and the price of a step depends neither on
which cell it touches nor on how large the value in that cell is.* Hold on to the second half.
The first half is the assumption people remember; the second half is the one Python breaks.

### A trace

Reverse `[7, 4, 9, 2]` in place, charging by the model. Two positions walk inwards and swap.

| Step | `left` | `right` | the list | charged | running total |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|---:|
| set up both positions | 0 | 3 | `[7, 4, 9, 2]` | 2 | 2 |
| test `0 < 3` — true | 0 | 3 | `[7, 4, 9, 2]` | 1 | 3 |
| swap: two reads, two writes | 0 | 3 | `[2, 4, 9, 7]` | 4 | 7 |
| advance: two adds, two writes | 1 | 2 | `[2, 4, 9, 7]` | 4 | 11 |
| test `1 < 2` — true | 1 | 2 | `[2, 4, 9, 7]` | 1 | 12 |
| swap: two reads, two writes | 1 | 2 | `[2, 9, 4, 7]` | 4 | 16 |
| advance: two adds, two writes | 2 | 1 | `[2, 9, 4, 7]` | 4 | 20 |
| test `2 < 1` — false, exit | 2 | 1 | `[2, 9, 4, 7]` | 1 | **21** |

Twenty-one. Not "linear" — twenty-one, a number, for this input. The whole business of
complexity begins here and consists of asking what that number does as the list gets longer, and
then throwing away everything about it that does not answer that question.

Look at the last row. The test that *fails* is charged. The model pays for asking, not only for
acting, and the loop that stops has paid for the question that told it to stop. Forgetting that
final test is the most common arithmetic slip in this kind of counting, and it is the reason the
formula in §7 has a `+ 3` in it rather than a `+ 2`.

## 6. Line by line

Counting on paper is fine for four elements. For anything else, make the program count itself.

```python
def reverse_in_place(xs: list[int]) -> int:
    """Reverse xs in place. Return the RAM-model operations charged."""
    left, right = 0, len(xs) - 1
    ops = 2
```

Charge 2 here: one for each position assigned. A stricter accountant would charge 4 — reading the
stored length is a step and subtracting one is another — and would be right. I am choosing to be
slightly sloppy in a place where sloppiness is provably harmless, and §7 says exactly why it is
harmless and exactly what that permission costs you. State the convention, then use it
consistently; an unstated convention is how two people's counts differ and neither can say why.

```python
    while left < right:
        ops += 1
```

The comparison is charged as its own step, before anything is done about the result. `<` and not
`<=`, for the reason 1.1 gave about palindromes: when `left == right` a single element would be
swapped with itself, which is real work in exchange for nothing.

```python
        xs[left], xs[right] = xs[right], xs[left]
        ops += 4
```

Four, not one. Two reads and two writes. This is the line where the model stops agreeing with
your eyes: Python lets you write a swap on one line, and one line is not one operation. The
brevity is a fact about Python's grammar. The four memory touches are a fact about the machine,
and the machine is what is being charged.

```python
        left, right = left + 1, right - 1
        ops += 4
```

Two additions and two assignments. Again the count follows the work, not the punctuation.

```python
    ops += 1
    return ops
```

The final comparison — the one that came back false and ended the loop. It happened, so it is
charged. Leave it out and every count you produce is short by exactly one, forever, which is the
kind of error that survives review because nobody checks a constant.

**The near-miss.** There are two, and the interesting thing is that only one of them matters.

*The harmless one.* Charge the swap as `1` instead of `4`, because it is one line. The total
becomes `3 + 6·⌊n/2⌋` instead of `3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋` — off by half as much again, on every input, at
every size. And §7 will show that it changes nothing anybody uses this count for. Sit with how
uncomfortable that is: a mistake of fifty per cent that the theory cannot see.

*The fatal one.* Charge something whose cost grows with `n` as though it were one step:

```python
def contains_counted(xs: list[int], target: int) -> tuple[bool, int]:
    return (target in xs), 1      # <- 'in' is not one operation
```

It reads as one operation because it is one word. It is a loop. §8 measures it, and the exact
output it prints is a lie about its own cost.

The rule that separates the two, and it is the rule to memorise from this document: **a step may
be charged one only when its cost is bounded by a constant that does not depend on `n`.** Get
that wrong by a constant factor and you have made an error the theory will forgive. Get it wrong
on something that grows and you have made an error that changes the exponent, which the theory
will not forgive and neither will production.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation.**

Let `n = len(xs)`, and let `k` be the number of times the loop body runs. `left` starts at `0`
and rises by one; `right` starts at `n - 1` and falls by one. They begin `n - 1` apart and close
by two per iteration, so the loop runs while they have not met: `k = ⌊n/2⌋`.

Outside the loop: 2 steps of setup, and 1 for the final failing test. Inside the body, per
iteration: 1 for the test that succeeded, 4 for the swap, 4 for the advance — 9 in all. So

```
T(n) = 2 + 1 + Σ (i = 1 … ⌊n/2⌋) 9
     = 3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋
```

Check it against something that can be run rather than believed. The function above, on lists of
length 0, 1, 2, 4, 5 and 8, returns 3, 3, 12, 21, 21 and 39. The formula gives 3, 3, 12, 21, 21,
39. A derivation you have not checked against an execution is a wish.

Dropping the constants: `T(n) = Θ(n)`. And now the promised point about the harmless near-miss —
charging the swap as 1 gives `3 + 6·⌊n/2⌋`, which is also `Θ(n)`. The two counts differ by 50%
at every size and land in the same class, because `Θ` deliberately discards exactly the
information that a constant factor carries. That is not a defect. It is the trade the notation
exists to make, and Day 3 is about why the trade is worth making. Its price is on display in
1.1 §7, where two `Θ(n)` procedures differed by a factor of thirty-four.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋` | any list of length `n` |
| Average | `3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋` | any list of length `n` |
| Worst | `3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋` | any list of length `n` |

All three rows are identical, and that is not laziness. This procedure has no branch that depends
on the *values* in the list — only on its length. Its execution path is decided before it looks
at any data. An algorithm with that property is called **oblivious**, and for an oblivious
algorithm best, average and worst case are the same number, so "average over what distribution"
stops being a question worth asking. Most procedures are not oblivious, which is what makes them
interesting and what makes their analysis hard. Today's `lab/` build has this property too, and
noticing *why* is the point of the exercise.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary — `left`, `right`, `ops`. The list is reversed in place, so nothing
proportional to `n` is allocated. Total space is `Θ(n)` because the input has to exist. Both
numbers are true; saying which one you mean is `CPX-12`, Day 8.

**What the constant hides.** The model charges 1 for `xs[left]`. On the machine this was written
on, that read costs about a nanosecond when the value is already in the processor's cache and
about a hundred times that when it is not — and the model has no vocabulary for the difference.
It also charges 1 for the interpreter's work in getting to the read at all, which in CPython is
considerably more than the read. Both are constant factors. Both are 2.3.

## 8. When it breaks

The model breaks the moment you charge one step for something that is a loop underneath. Here is
the counter from §6, reporting its own cost:

```
>>> contains_counted(list(range(10_000_000)), -1)
(False, 1)
```

One operation, it says, on ten million elements. Now measure it — a list of `n` random integers,
searching for a value that is not present, so the scan cannot stop early; the best of seven runs
at each size, on CPython 3.12.10, an Intel Core i3-1115G4:

```
         n      seconds     ratio
    100000     0.000521         -
    200000     0.001118      2.15
    400000     0.002407      2.15
    800000     0.004638      1.93
   1600000     0.009864      2.13
   3200000     0.021942      2.22
   6400000     0.054652      2.49
```

Read the ratio column, not the seconds. A cost of 1 predicts a ratio of `1.00` — doubling the
list should change nothing. The measurement says every doubling of `n` doubles the time. `in` is
`Θ(n)`, the counter is not off by a constant, it is off by a factor of `n`, and at ten million
elements it has understated the truth ten-million-fold.

Two things worth noticing in that table before leaving it. The final ratio is `2.49` rather than
`2.1` — the list has grown past what the processor can keep close by, and the model's second
assumption is beginning to fail in public. That is 2.3. And the `1.93` at 800,000 is not the
computer being sublinear; it is measurement noise, which is why the doubling harness in every
`lab/` takes seven runs and reports the smallest.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** Charging `in` as `n` fixes this line. What fixes the
next line is the rule from §6, applied as a habit: *before charging any step 1, ask whether its
cost can grow with the input.* In Python the answers that surprise people are worth learning
now — `x in a_list` is `Θ(n)`; `a_list.insert(0, x)` is `Θ(n)`; `a_list[1:]` is `Θ(n)` and
allocates; `s += t` inside a loop is `Θ(n²)` over the loop; `min(xs)` and `sum(xs)` are `Θ(n)`
however short they look. Every one of these is one word, and one word is not one step. The
curriculum re-derives each of them from its own implementation on the day that owns it —
`ARR-06` on Day 18 for the insert, `STR-03` on Day 22 for the string concatenation — and each
time the derivation will feel familiar, because it is this rule again.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** At scale the RAM model is not patched. It is replaced — and knowing *which*
replacement a system uses tells you almost everything about its design.

When the data does not fit in memory, the thing worth counting is not operations but **block
transfers**, because one trip to disk costs more than a hundred thousand operations and the trips
are what you are actually buying. That is the external-memory model, and you can read its
constants directly out of a running PostgreSQL: `seq_page_cost` defaults to `1.0`,
`random_page_cost` to `4.0`, and `cpu_tuple_cost` to `0.01`. In that model, processing a row is
priced at one hundredth of reading a page sequentially, and a random page read costs four times a
sequential one. Teams running on SSDs routinely lower `random_page_cost` to about `1.1`, which is
a cost model being retuned for hardware, in a config file, by hand. It is also why a B-tree node
holds hundreds of keys instead of two: under a model that counts block transfers, a fat shallow
tree beats a thin deep one, and under the RAM model that choice makes no sense at all. `SYS-05`,
Day 213.

When the data is spread across machines, the unit changes again — to round trips and to bytes
moved across the network. A Spark job's cost is dominated by shuffle bytes, and no amount of
counting operations will find the problem in one.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "The counter's fine but it's charging `in` as one step, so the number it returns is fiction and
> the dashboard built on it is worse than nothing. Either count it properly or drop the counter
> and let the profiler tell us. And put the charging convention in the docstring — you and I will
> disagree about whether a swap is one op or four, and I'd rather we disagree in writing."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you have given a correct complexity:

1. *"On what machine?"* — checking whether "each operation costs one" is a stated assumption you
   own or a habit you inherited. The follow-up, when you answer, is whether the assumption holds
   in the language you just wrote the code in.
2. *"You called that comparison one operation. What if the elements are strings?"* — checking
   whether you noticed that comparing two length-`m` strings is `Θ(m)`, and that `n log n`
   comparisons is therefore `Θ(m · n log n)` steps. Most people have never priced the comparison.
3. *"Your two candidate solutions are both `Θ(n)`. How do you choose?"* — checking whether you
   understand that the model has run out of things to say, and that the honest next move is to
   measure rather than to argue.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. State the three assumptions of the RAM model.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Each basic operation costs one step; memory is a numbered row of cells, any of which is
   reachable in one step regardless of its address; one cell holds one number, of at most `w`
   bits, with `w` large enough to hold an index into the input.
   </details>

2. Why does the model charge for the loop test that fails?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because it happened. The processor evaluated the comparison and acted on the result; the fact
   that the result was "stop" does not make the comparison free. Concretely it is the difference
   between `3 + 9·⌊n/2⌋` and `2 + 9·⌊n/2⌋`, which matters not at all to the `Θ` and quite a lot to
   whether you can be trusted with an exact count.
   </details>

3. `reverse_in_place` reports the same operation count for `[1, 2, 3, 4]` and `[9, 9, 9, 9]`. Why,
   and what is that property called?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The procedure never branches on a value — only on the positions `left` and `right`, whose whole
   history is fixed by `len(xs)`. The execution path is determined before any element is read.
   Such an algorithm is **oblivious**, and for it, best, average and worst case coincide.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** Write a two-line Python function whose reported RAM-model cost is `Θ(1)` and
   whose real cost is `Θ(n²)`.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   ```python
   def build(xs: list[int]) -> tuple[str, int]:
       out = ""
       for x in xs:
           out += str(x)     # each += copies the whole accumulated string
       return out, len(xs)   # reported cost: one step per element
   ```

   Reported `Θ(n)`, actually `Θ(n²)`, because each `+=` on an immutable string builds a fresh copy
   of everything accumulated so far, so the copying sums to `1 + 2 + … + n`. Same trap as
   `contains_counted`, one level nastier: here the step being mispriced is not just non-constant,
   its price *grows as the loop proceeds*. `STR-03`, Day 22.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-01`.** 1.2 said a specification has no complexity. This document says every
   complexity claim is about a machine. Put the two together into one sentence about what a
   complexity claim actually names.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   A complexity claim names a *procedure* measured on a *stated machine* — it needs both, and it
   is meaningless if either is missing. "Sorting is `Θ(n log n)`" fails on the first count (which
   procedure?) and interviewers let it pass daily. "This merge sort is `Θ(n log n)`" fails on the
   second when the elements are long strings and the comparison you charged 1 for is really
   `Θ(m)`.
   </details>

6. **In review.** A colleague's benchmark shows their `Θ(n log n)` implementation beating their
   `Θ(n)` one at every size they tried. What are the two possible explanations, and what do you
   ask for?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Either the constant factor of the linear version is large enough to lose over the range tested
   — entirely possible, and 1.1 §7 has a factor of thirty-four to prove it — or one of the two
   complexity claims is wrong because a step was mispriced. Ask for the range of `n`, and for the
   ratio column rather than the wall clock. If the linear version's ratio sits near 2 per doubling
   and the other near 2.1, both claims are right and the constant is simply winning, which is
   information, not an error. If the "linear" version's ratio is near 4, the claim was wrong.
   </details>
