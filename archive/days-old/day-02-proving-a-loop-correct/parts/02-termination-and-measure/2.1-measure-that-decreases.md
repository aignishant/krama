# 2.1 — The measure that decreases

## 1. The one-line answer

A loop stops when you can name a whole number that is never negative while the loop is running
and is strictly smaller after every pass through the body, because nothing can fall forever in
whole steps and stay above zero.

---

## 2. The story

Gate 24B at Kolkata, and the flight is full in the way that only a Sunday evening flight is full.

Debasish Roy has the passenger list on a clipboard and a problem on the jet bridge. A family of
four is spread across the cabin in single seats. A passenger travelling after surgery has a window
seat at the back and a letter from a doctor asking for an aisle at the front. Two crew travelling
on duty have been given seats that must be free for the cabin manager. Eleven people are sitting
where they should not be, and the aircraft cannot push back until none of them are.

There is a way to do this that does not work, and Debasish learned it as a trainee. You start
moving people. You ask 14C to take 22F, and then discover that 22F's occupant has to go to 9A, and
9A has to go to 14C, and you have spent the whole of boarding walking three people around a
triangle. Nothing is wrong with any single move. Every one of them was reasonable. The aircraft is
simply no closer to leaving than when you started, and the ground staff are announcing a departure
slot that has already gone.

So there is a rule, and it is the only rule the supervisor cared about: **never make an
announcement unless it leaves fewer people in the wrong seat than there were before it.** Not the
same number. Fewer. If a move cannot be justified that way it does not get made, however sensible
it looks.

The number lives in the corner of the clipboard, and Debasish crosses it out and rewrites it after
every announcement. Eleven. Nine. Eight. Six. It is allowed to fall by more than one — a family of
four resolved in a single swap is a good evening — but it is never allowed to stay where it is,
and it can never go below zero, because you cannot have fewer than nobody in the wrong seat.

That is the whole of why this ends. Not the skill of the moves. Not the goodwill of the
passengers. The number started at eleven, it goes down every time, and it stops at nothing. There
is no version of this evening in which Debasish is still making announcements at midnight, because
there is no way to subtract from eleven forever.

The doors close. The number on the clipboard is zero, and the crossings-out above it are the
proof.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

The number on the clipboard is a **variant** — also called a termination measure, or a decreasing
measure. It is not the invariant. The invariant of
[1.1](../01-invariants-and-induction/1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) is a claim about what
is *true*; the variant is a claim about what is *shrinking*. A loop needs both, and they are
proved separately.

Three things are required of it, and each of them is doing work.

**It is a whole number.** Not a distance, not a fraction, not a float. Something that decreases
strictly can still decrease forever if it is allowed to take smaller and smaller steps: 1, ½, ¼, ⅛
— always going down, never reaching the bottom, never stopping. Whole numbers cannot do that.
Every step down is at least a full step. (The general condition is that the measure lives in a
*well-founded* ordering — one with no infinite descending chain. The natural numbers are the one
you will use for years, so use them.)

**It never goes below zero while the guard is true.** This is a claim about the loop's
precondition as much as about the loop. Eleven misplaced passengers cannot become minus one. §8 is
a loop whose measure *can* go negative for some inputs, and the result is not a wrong answer — it
is a program that never comes back.

**Every iteration makes it strictly smaller.** This is the obligation the triangle of reseated
passengers fails. Each move was fine; the measure did not fall; the loop had no reason to stop.

Put the three together and the conclusion arrives with a number attached: if the measure starts at
`V₀` and falls by at least one each time, the body runs at most `V₀` times. Termination and a
running-time bound are not two arguments. They are one argument, read twice.

One warning about where to look for it. The variant is almost never a variable of the program. It
is usually an *expression over the program's state*: `len(xs) - i`, `hi - lo`, `remainder`, the
number of unvisited nodes, the number of passengers in the wrong seat. Asking "which variable is
the variant" is the wrong question, and it is why people conclude a loop has none.

> **Formally:** let `V` be a function from the loop's state to the natural numbers. If `V(s) ≥ 0`
> for every state `s` in which the guard `B` holds, and every execution of the body from a state
> `s` satisfying `B` reaches a state `s'` with `V(s') < V(s)`, then the loop terminates, and its
> body executes at most `V(s₀)` times, where `s₀` is the state at the loop's first test.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **Dafny, and the `decreases` clause.** Dafny is a language whose compiler refuses a program it
  cannot prove correct, and every loop and every recursive function in it carries a `decreases`
  expression — the variant, written into the source as a first-class part of the code. The verifier
  guesses one for the easy cases (a loop running `i` up to `n` gets `n - i` automatically) and
  fails the build when it cannot, at which point a human has to supply the sentence. Amazon's s2n
  TLS library and parts of AWS's authorisation engine are verified this way. The reason the clause
  exists as syntax rather than as a comment is exactly today's lesson: the invariant and the
  variant are different obligations, and a tool that checks one cannot infer the other.

- **The Ethereum Virtual Machine's gas counter.** Every instruction a contract executes subtracts a
  fixed amount from a counter that started at whatever the sender paid for, and the machine halts
  when the counter would go below zero. That counter is a variant imposed by the platform rather
  than derived from the program — the EVM cannot decide, in general, whether an arbitrary
  contract's loop terminates, so it does not try. It attaches a non-negative integer that strictly
  decreases to *every* step and makes non-termination impossible by construction. The cost is that
  a perfectly correct long computation dies halfway with `out of gas`; the benefit is that no
  contract can wedge the network. When you cannot prove a variant you can sometimes impose one, and
  every timeout you have ever configured is that same move.

- **Asked as:** *"Is there an input for which this loops forever?"* Usually asked about code the
  interviewer wrote, in a review-the-diff exercise. The answer they want is not "let me trace it" —
  it is "the measure here is `hi - lo`, and it decreases as long as `mid` is strictly inside the
  range; on a two-element range with this arithmetic it isn't, so yes: `lo = 0, hi = 2`."

## 5. The mechanism

Take the shortest loop with a measure worth naming: how many times can you halve a number before
you reach one?

```
    V = n

    25 |*
       |
    12 |    *
       |
     6 |        *
       |
     3 |            *
       |
     1 |                *   <- guard n > 1 is now false; stop
       |
     0 +----+---+---+---+----
         0   1   2   3   4     iterations completed
```

*What to notice: the steps are not equal. The measure falls by thirteen, then six, then three,
then two. The proof does not care. It requires only that every step goes down and that the floor is
real — an argument that needed equal steps would be an argument about arithmetic, and this one is
an argument about order.*

**The invariant:** *`n` is a positive integer at the top of every iteration, and `count` is the
number of halvings performed so far.*

**The variant:** *`n` itself — non-negative while the guard holds, and strictly smaller at the top
of each iteration than it was at the top of the one before.*

Two claims, two sentences, and the second one is the one that says this loop ends.

### A trace

Input `n = 25`. State recorded at the **top** of each iteration.

| At the top of | `n` | `count` | Guard `n > 1` | Variant `V = n` |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| iteration 1 | 25 | 0 | true | 25 |
| iteration 2 | 12 | 1 | true | 12 |
| iteration 3 | 6 | 2 | true | 6 |
| iteration 4 | 3 | 3 | true | 3 |
| after the loop | 1 | 4 | **false** | 1 |

The variant column is monotone downwards and never negative, and that pair of facts is the whole
termination proof. Note also what the bound gives you: the measure started at 25, so the body runs
at most 25 times. That is true, and it is terrible — the loop actually runs four times. A variant
proves *that* a loop stops; whether it also tells you *when* depends on how tightly it tracks the
work, and §7 is where the loose bound gets replaced by a sharp one.

## 6. Line by line

```python
def halvings_until_one(n: int) -> int:
    if n < 1:
        raise ValueError("halvings_until_one() requires n >= 1")
```

The precondition comes first, and it is not defensive programming — it is the domain on which the
variant is well-founded. For `n >= 1` the measure `n` is bounded below by one and the argument goes
through. For `n = 0` and for negatives it is not, and §8 is what that costs. Whenever you raise on
a precondition, ask which of your two proofs would have failed without it; if the answer is
"neither", the check is decoration.

```python
    count = 0
    while n > 1:
```

The guard is `n > 1`, not `n != 1`. Those are the same test for every input this function accepts
and wildly different tests for the inputs it does not, which is the most common way a correct loop
becomes an infinite one. A guard written as an inequality against the measure's floor fails safe:
whatever `n` is, `n > 1` stops being true once `n` has fallen far enough. A guard written as an
equality only stops if the measure lands exactly on the value you named.

```python
        n //= 2
        count += 1
```

Two lines discharging two different obligations. `n //= 2` is the variant step: it is the only line
in the body that touches the measure, and it must strictly decrease it. `count += 1` is the
maintenance step for the invariant, in the sense
[1.1](../01-invariants-and-induction/1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) means. Being able to
point at which line is doing which job is most of what this day teaches.

Why `//=` and not `/= 2`? Because true division produces a float, and the moment the measure is a
float it is allowed to descend forever: `1.0, 0.5, 0.25, …`. With this particular guard it would
still stop, which is worse than failing outright — it would stop for reasons unconnected to the
argument you wrote down. Keep the measure in the integers, where the proof lives.

```python
    return count
```

The loop stopped because `n > 1` went false, so `n <= 1`, and with the precondition and the
invariant that means `n == 1` exactly. That is the negated-guard step from
[1.2](../01-invariants-and-induction/1.2-exit-and-negated-guard.md), and it is worth noticing that
it is *still* a separate argument. The variant told you the loop ends. It said nothing about the
answer being right.

**The near-miss.** Here is the same function with the guard written the way people say it out loud
— "keep halving until it *is* one":

```python
def halvings_until_one_eq(n: int) -> int:
    count = 0
    while n != 1:
        n //= 2
        count += 1
    return count
```

For every `n >= 1` this is identical, and it will pass every test you are likely to write. Then
somebody passes zero, or a negative:

```
>>> n = -3
>>> n //= 2; n
-2
>>> n //= 2; n
-1
>>> n //= 2; n
-1
>>> n //= 2; n
-1
```

Python's `//` rounds towards negative infinity, so `-1 // 2` is `-1`, not `0`. The measure stops
descending, and it stops one short of the value the guard is testing for. With `n = 0` it is worse
and simpler: `0 // 2` is `0`, and the loop spins on a value that never changes at all. The
inequality guard survives both, because it does not need the measure to hit a target — it needs
only that the measure eventually gets below one, and both `0` and `-1` already are.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation.**

The body is a fixed amount of work `c` per iteration — one comparison, one shift, one increment. So
the whole question is how many iterations there are, and that is a question about the variant.

Let `n₀` be the starting value. After `i` iterations the value is

```
n_i = floor(n₀ / 2^i)
```

which holds because repeated floor division composes exactly: `floor(floor(x / 2) / 2)` is
`floor(x / 4)` for every positive integer `x`. The guard fails at the first `i` for which
`n_i <= 1`, so the body runs

```
i = floor(log₂ n₀)   times

T(n₀) = Σ (j = 1 … floor(log₂ n₀)) c  =  c · floor(log₂ n₀)
```

Check it against the trace: `floor(log₂ 25)` is 4, and the trace ran four iterations.

Notice what just happened to the bound from §5. The crude reading of the variant — "it starts at
`V₀` and falls by at least one, so at most `V₀` iterations" — gave 25. The sharp reading, which
uses *how* the measure falls rather than only *that* it falls, gives 4. Both are correct. A variant
that decreases by a constant amount bounds the work by the measure itself; a variant that decreases
by a constant *factor* bounds it by the number of digits of the measure. That gap is the difference
between the two most common shapes in this curriculum, and Day 3 is where the two shapes get their
names.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `c · floor(log₂ n)` | Any `n >= 1` |
| Average | `c · floor(log₂ n)` | Any `n >= 1` |
| Worst | `c · floor(log₂ n)` | Any `n >= 1` |

Identical rows again, and for the reason
[1.1](../01-invariants-and-induction/1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) gave: no early exit, no
data-dependent branch, so the operation count is a function of `n` alone. The loop is oblivious.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary — two integers, and no recursion stack, because there is no recursion.

**What the constant hides — and here it hides something that changes the class.** The derivation
charged `n //= 2` as one operation. That is the RAM model of `CPX-02` speaking, and Day 1's 2.3
warned where it leaks: Python integers are arbitrary precision, so halving a `b`-bit integer copies
about `b/64` machine words. Each iteration costs proportional to the current width, and the widths
run `b, b-1, b-2, …`:

```
Σ (j = 1 … b) j / 64  =  b(b + 1) / 128  =  Θ(b²)
```

Measured, CPython 3.12.12, best of five runs, `n = 2^bits - 1`:

```
bits=  1000  halvings=   999  best=    0.145 ms
bits=  2000  halvings=  1999  best=    0.462 ms
bits=  4000  halvings=  3999  best=    1.641 ms
bits=  8000  halvings=  7999  best=    6.429 ms
bits= 16000  halvings= 15999  best=   25.074 ms
```

Ratios of 3.2, 3.6, 3.9, 3.9 — approaching 4 as `bits` doubles, which is the signature of `b²` and
not of `b`. The iteration count is exactly linear in `bits`; the *time* is quadratic in it. Both
statements are true, they are about different models, and only one of them is about your machine.
This is Day 1's trial division priced in digits, arriving from the other direction: the variant
counts iterations, and iterations are not seconds.

## 8. When it breaks

Run the near-miss on a negative input and there is no error, no output, and no end. What you get is
whatever you type next — here, an interrupt after half a second:

```
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "d2b.py", line 14, in <module>
    print(halvings_until_one(-3))
          ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
  File "d2b.py", line 7, in halvings_until_one
    while n != 1:
          ^^^^^^
KeyboardInterrupt
```

Read where the traceback points. Not at a line that did something wrong — at `while n != 1`, the
line that was *doing nothing*, over and over, at the moment the interrupt arrived. That is the
signature of a failed variant, and it is worth memorising, because it is the only failure in this
curriculum that produces no diagnostic of its own. The exception in a non-terminating program is
always about the interruption, never about the bug. Note also that the process had to be stopped by
hand: exit status 130, no assertion, no test failure, nothing a CI job reports except a build that
never finished.

What went wrong is not the guard, although changing the guard fixes it. It is that the function
accepted an input on which its measure is not well-founded. For `n = -3` the values run
`-2, -1, -1, -1, …`: bounded below, yes, but not strictly decreasing, so obligation three fails.
For `n = 0` the values run `0, 0, 0, …` and it fails harder. In both cases the loop is doing exactly
what it was told, forever.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is `while n > 1`. It works, and you should
write it. The fix is the habit: **before writing a loop, write down the measure and the domain on
which it is well-founded, then make the function refuse inputs outside that domain.** The
`raise ValueError` in §6 is not there to be polite to callers. It is there because the termination
proof has a precondition, and an unchecked precondition is a proof about a function other than the
one you shipped. Every one of the seven days of binary search in Phase 7 will fail in this exact
shape at least once — a range that stops shrinking because `mid` computes back to `lo` — and the
people who find it in a minute rather than an evening are the ones who wrote `hi - lo` on paper
before they wrote the `while`.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** Three changes, and the first is a reversal.

*Most industrial loops are supposed to run forever, so the question moves.* A Kubernetes
controller, a database's autovacuum daemon, an event loop, a game loop: none of these terminate,
and demanding a variant for them is a category error. What you demand instead is that each *pass*
has one — bounded work per reconcile, a work queue that drains, a batch size that caps — so that
the non-terminating outer loop is built out of terminating inner ones. When an operator says "the
controller is hot-looping", they mean an inner pass whose measure stopped decreasing: it dequeues an
item, fails, requeues it, and does that forever at full CPU. The repair in that world is almost
always to add an artificial component to the measure — a retry count, an exponential backoff, a
deadline — because the natural one turned out not to decrease.

*Well-foundedness is not free once you leave the integers.* Ford–Fulkerson is the famous casualty:
with irrational edge capacities every iteration strictly increases the flow, and the algorithm
still never terminates, because a strictly increasing bounded sequence of reals is under no
obligation to reach its limit. That is `FLW-03` on Day 152, and it is the cleanest proof in the
curriculum that "the measure gets better every time" is not a termination argument. Real
implementations keep capacities integral for precisely this reason.

*When you cannot prove it, you impose it.* PostgreSQL has `statement_timeout`. HTTP clients have
deadlines. Backtracking regex engines — the ReDoS family — are increasingly replaced by engines
that cannot backtrack, or wrapped in a step budget. Each of those is a variant bolted on from
outside, and each converts an unbounded-time failure into a loud, catchable one. That conversion is
not a workaround; it is the difference between a page that renders an error and a thread pool that
fills up and takes the service down with it.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "What's the measure here? I can see the invariant and I believe it, but I've read this `while`
> three times and I can't tell you what is smaller at the end of the body than at the start. If the
> answer is `hi - lo`, put that in a comment, because on a two-element range I don't think it is.
> And the `!=` in the guard — make it `<`. I don't care that the values are always positive today.
> Guards that test equality against a moving quantity are how we get a 3 a.m. page with a thread
> pinned at 100% and no traceback to read."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you state the invariant and the loop is correct:

1. *"Name the quantity that decreases, and say what its floor is."* — checking whether you know a
   variant is an expression over the state rather than a variable. Candidates who answer "`i` goes
   up" have not noticed that the measure is `n - i`.
2. *"Your measure decreases by at least one each iteration and starts at `n`. Does that make the
   loop linear?"* — checking whether you can tell a termination bound from a tight bound. It makes
   it *at most* linear; the halving loop's measure also starts at `n`, and it is not linear.
3. *"Suppose the measure is a float that always decreases. Does the loop terminate?"* — checking
   whether you know why the naturals were in the definition. No: `1, ½, ¼, …`. This is
   Ford–Fulkerson in one line, and a candidate who reaches for it has read something.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. State the three obligations on a variant, and say which one the reseating triangle in the story
   violated.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   It is a whole number (well-founded — no infinite descending chain); it is never negative while
   the guard holds; every iteration strictly decreases it. The triangle satisfied the first two — a
   count of misplaced passengers is a non-negative integer — and violated the third: three people
   moved and the count was unchanged afterwards. Every individual move looked like progress and
   none of it was.
   </details>

2. Where is the variant of `for i in range(len(xs)):`, given that nothing in it decreases?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `len(xs) - i`. The variant is an expression over the state, not a variable in the source. This is
   also why `for` loops over a fixed range feel like they need no termination argument — the
   language wrote the measure for you — and it is exactly why mutating `xs` inside such a loop is a
   hazard: you are editing someone else's variant.
   </details>

3. One loop's measure decreases by at least one per iteration and starts at `n`. Another's starts at
   `n` and halves each iteration. Both terminate. What is different about what you have proved?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Only the bound. Termination is established identically for both — a non-negative integer that
   strictly decreases. The first argument bounds the work at `n` iterations, the second at
   `floor(log₂ n)`, because the second used *how much* the measure falls rather than merely that it
   falls. §7 makes this concrete: the crude reading of the halving loop's variant gives 25
   iterations for `n = 25`, the sharp reading gives 4.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** Here is a loop whose invariant is impeccable and whose measure decreases on every
   input you are likely to try. Find the inputs on which it never returns, and name which obligation
   fails for each.

   ```python
   def steps_to_zero(x: int, step: int) -> int:
       count = 0
       while x != 0:
           x -= step
           count += 1
       return count
   ```
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `steps_to_zero(7, 2)` never returns: `x` runs `5, 3, 1, -1, -3, …` and steps straight past the
   target. `steps_to_zero(5, 0)` never returns either, and `steps_to_zero(5, -1)` runs away upwards.
   Which obligation fails depends on the input, which is the interesting part. With `step = 2` the
   measure `abs(x)` does decrease for a while and then starts increasing, so obligation three fails
   partway through. With `step = 0` it fails immediately. With a negative `step` the measure has no
   floor at all and obligation two fails. All three are repaired by the same two moves — an
   inequality guard (`while x > 0`) and a precondition (`step >= 1`) — which is the §8 lesson twice.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** §7 derived `floor(log₂ n)` iterations and then measured a quadratic
   running time. Explain the disagreement using Day 1's rule about what may be charged as one
   operation, and say what `n` is in each of the two statements.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The derivation charged `n //= 2` as one step, which is legitimate only if its cost does not grow
   with the input. For a Python `int` it does — halving a `b`-bit integer touches about `b/64`
   machine words — so the charge is wrong and the model leaks. In the iteration count `n` is the
   *value*; in the measurement the size that matters is `b`, the number of bits, and `b` is about
   `log₂ n`. So "logarithmic in the value" and "linear in the size" are the same statement, and the
   measured time is `Θ(b²)` because there are `b` halvings each costing `Θ(b)`. This is Day 1's
   trial division priced in digits, read backwards.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-01`.** Day 1 listed finiteness as a property of an algorithm. Which of today's
   two obligations — invariant or variant — establishes it, and what does that say about a procedure
   that satisfies the other one perfectly?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The variant. Initialization and maintenance are conditional claims — *if* control reaches the top
   of an iteration, then `P` holds — and a loop that never leaves satisfies them for eternity
   without producing anything. A procedure with a flawless invariant and no variant is not a slow
   algorithm; by Day 1's definition it is not an algorithm at all. That distinction has a name, and
   it is [2.2](2.2-partial-versus-total-correctness.md).
   </details>

7. **In review.** A colleague submits `while n != 1: n = n // 2 if n % 2 == 0 else 3 * n + 1` with
   the comment `# always terminates`. What do you say?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   That the comment is not known to be true. This is the Collatz iteration, and whether it reaches
   one for every positive integer is an open problem — checked by machine past 2⁶⁸, proved for
   nothing. No variant is known. So the review comment is not "prove it" but "bound it": if this
   ships, it ships with a step cap and a documented behaviour when the cap is hit, because an
   unbounded loop in production is an outage waiting for the right input. It is also the honest
   answer to "can you always find the measure" — no, and people have been looking for this one since
   the 1930s.
   </details>
