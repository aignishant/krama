# 1.2 — Exit, and the negated guard

## 1. The one-line answer

A loop's result is justified by two facts together — what stayed true throughout, and the reason
the loop stopped — and using only the first is how correct-looking code gets the wrong answer.

---

## 2. The story

The cane is eight feet high and it has been dark for two hours.

Anjali Kadam is seven and she was last seen near the pump house at dusk. By nine o'clock there
are sixty people at the edge of her family's field, most of them holding torches, some holding
nothing at all because they came straight from wherever they were when the news reached them.

Baburao Shinde, who is the sarpanch and who has done this twice before, makes them stand in a
line. Shoulder to shoulder, an arm's length apart, along the whole southern edge. He says one
thing, twice, so that everyone hears it: *nobody moves ahead of the line, and nobody stays
behind it.* Then they walk north together, torches sweeping, calling her name.

The reason for the line is not that it is thorough. The reason is that it makes one sentence true
and keeps it true: **the cane behind us has been looked at, and she is not in it.** Every step the
line takes, the sentence covers more ground. If a man drifts forward because he is anxious, he
opens a gap that nobody will ever go back to, and the sentence quietly stops being true — and the
terrible thing is that it stops being true *silently*. Nothing looks different. The line still
moves. It just no longer means anything.

An hour in, they reach the irrigation channel. It is four feet across, full, and the far bank is
mud. The line stops. Somebody says what half of them are thinking: she is not in this field,
somebody should take a motorcycle down to the Beed road.

Baburao says no. Not because he thinks she is in the cane. Because of what the line has actually
earned. It has walked from the southern edge to the channel. Everything they can say is about
that strip of ground. The field does not end at the channel — there are two hundred metres of
cane on the other side of it, up to the fence, and no torch has been near them. To say *she is not
in the field* they need to have stopped at the fence. They stopped at the water.

Two men go back for planks. It takes twenty minutes to get the line across, and it re-forms on the
far bank, shoulder to shoulder, and starts north again.

They find her forty metres short of the fence, asleep, curled in the space between two rows, with
her hands over her ears against the noise of everyone shouting her name.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

The line is a loop. Each step north is one iteration. The sentence Baburao keeps true — *the cane
behind the line has been looked at, and she is not in it* — is the **invariant**, and
[1.1](1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) is the whole of how it is kept.

What this document is about is the moment the line stops.

An invariant, on its own, licenses nothing. *She is not in the cane behind us* is true at the
southern edge, when "behind us" is nothing at all. It is true at the channel. It would be true if
the line took one step and gave up. The claim gets stronger as the loop runs, but it never becomes
the answer, because it is always a statement about the part that is done, and never about the
part that is left.

The answer arrives only when you add the second fact: **why the loop stopped**. A loop stops
because its guard went false. Stopping at the fence means there is no unsearched cane left, and
*that* sentence — there is none left — is what turns *she is not in the searched part* into *she
is not in the field*. Neither sentence gets there alone. You need the conjunction.

So the shape of every loop proof is the same, and it has exactly three moving parts:

- the invariant `P`, true at the top of every iteration,
- the guard `B`, which was false at the moment the loop exited,
- and the postcondition `Q` — what the caller was promised.

The obligation is that `P ∧ ¬B` implies `Q`. Baburao's objection, translated: the invariant was
impeccable, but `¬B` was "we have reached the channel", and "we have reached the channel" does not
imply "there is no unsearched field". The proof does not go through, and no amount of care about
the invariant repairs it, because the flaw is not in the invariant.

A loop with a second exit — a `return` in the middle, a `break` — has a second obligation of the
same shape, with a different `¬B`. Finding Anjali stops the line too, and the conclusion at that
exit is not *she is not in the field*; it is *here she is*. Two exits, two conclusions, two
proofs. Code that leaves a loop by two doors and hands both results to the same line underneath is
where this goes wrong in practice.

> **Formally:** for a loop `while B: S` with invariant `P` and postcondition `Q`, partial
> correctness requires that `P` be established before the loop, that `{P ∧ B} S {P}` hold for the
> body, and that `P ∧ ¬B ⇒ Q`. Every additional exit point `e` carries its own obligation
> `P ∧ C_e ⇒ Q`, where `C_e` is the condition under which control leaves by that door.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **`git bisect`.** The invariant is exactly one sentence: *the first bad commit lies inside the
  range between the known-good and the known-bad end*. Every test you run halves that range and
  re-establishes the sentence. The loop's guard is *the range still contains more than one
  candidate*, and it is the negation of that guard — the range has collapsed to a single commit —
  that converts the invariant into `git bisect`'s answer. This is why `git bisect skip` is
  dangerous in a way that `git bisect good` is not: skipping leaves commits inside the range that
  were never tested, so the range collapses without the invariant covering everything in it, and
  the commit git names at the end is a guess. It is the man drifting ahead of the line.

- **Kubernetes readiness probes.** A probe loop leaves by two doors: the endpoint answered, or the
  deadline passed. The invariant — *no successful response has been seen yet* — is identical at
  both. Only the exit condition distinguishes "this container is not ready" from "this container
  did not answer us in time", and those are different facts about the world: the second happens
  when a node is under CPU pressure and the process is perfectly healthy. Clusters that treat the
  two exits as one door kill healthy pods under load, which removes capacity, which increases
  load. Same invariant, different `¬B`, and the difference is an outage.

- **Asked as:** *"Your function returns `-1` at the bottom. Convince me that's right."* The
  answer that gets marked down re-describes the loop. The answer they want is two clauses: the
  invariant says the target is not in the part already scanned, and the loop only reaches this
  line when the unscanned part is empty — so the target is nowhere.

## 5. The mechanism

The smallest loop with two doors: find the position of a value, or report that it is absent.

```
                                       i = 3
                                         |
                                         v
    index        0     1     2     3     4     5
              +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
    xs        |  4  |  7  |  2  |  9  |  1  |  5  |
              +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
               \_______________/ \_________________/
                looked at, and    not looked at
                target is not
                in here
```

*Notice that the picture shows the invariant and says nothing whatever about the answer. The
shaded region can grow all the way to the right-hand edge and the invariant will still only be
telling you where the target **is not**. That is the point: the answer is not in the picture, it
is in the moment the right-hand region becomes empty.*

**The invariant:** *`target` does not appear anywhere in `xs[0:i]`.*

**The guard:** `i < len(xs)`. Its negation, at the bottom of the loop, is `i == len(xs)`, which
makes `xs[0:i]` the whole list — and *that* is the step that produces the answer.

### A trace

Input `[4, 7, 2]`, `target = 9`, which is absent — the case where the exit reasoning does all the
work.

| At the top of | `i` | Guard `i < 3` | Invariant: `9 ∉ xs[0:i]` | |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| iteration 1 | 0 | true | `9 ∉ []` ✓ | vacuous |
| iteration 2 | 1 | true | `9 ∉ [4]` ✓ | |
| iteration 3 | 2 | true | `9 ∉ [4, 7]` ✓ | |
| the exit | 3 | **false** | `9 ∉ [4, 7, 2]` ✓ | ← `¬B` makes this the whole list |

Read the last row twice. The invariant in that row is not stronger than the invariant in the row
above it — it is the same sentence, mechanically extended by one more element. What changed is the
guard, and the guard is what makes `xs[0:i]` stop being *a prefix* and start being *everything*.

## 6. Line by line

```python
def index_of(xs: list[int], target: int) -> int:
    i = 0
```

Initialization: `xs[0:0]` is empty, and `target` is not in an empty list. True vacuously, in the
sense [1.1](1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) insisted was load-bearing rather than a dodge.

```python
    while i < len(xs):
        if xs[i] == target:
            return i
```

The first door. Note what is required to justify it, because it is *not* the invariant — it is the
condition under which we leave: `xs[i] == target`. The obligation here is `xs[i] == target ⇒ i is
a valid answer`, which is immediate. The invariant is not even needed at this exit; it is needed
at the other one. Two doors, two arguments, and this is the easy door.

```python
        i += 1
```

Maintenance. We reached this line only because `xs[i] != target`, so extending the scanned prefix
by one element preserves *`target` is not in `xs[0:i]`*. The `if` above is not a shortcut bolted
onto the loop — it is precisely the case analysis that makes this increment legal.

```python
    return -1
```

The second door, and the whole subject of this document. Control gets here only when the guard is
false, so `i == len(xs)`, so `xs[0:i]` is all of `xs`, so — by the invariant — `target` is nowhere
in `xs`. `P ∧ ¬B ⇒ Q`. That is three short steps and every one of them is doing work; drop any one
and the line is unjustified.

**The near-miss.** Here is an "optimisation" that is completely correct, and completely correct
for a reason the code does not state:

```python
def index_of_stopping_early(xs: list[int], target: int) -> int:
    i = 0
    while i < len(xs) and xs[i] <= target:   # stop as soon as we pass it
        if xs[i] == target:
            return i
        i += 1
    return -1
```

On a sorted list this is genuinely better — once you are past `target` there is no point looking
further. And the invariant is untouched: `target` still is not in `xs[0:i]` at every step.

```
sorted   [1, 3, 9], find 3 : 1
sorted   [1, 3, 9], find 5 : -1
unsorted [9, 3],    find 3 : -1
correct  [9, 3],    find 3 : 1
```

The third line is the bug. `3` is at index 1 and the function says it is absent.

Now look at where the flaw actually is, because this is the lesson of the document in one example.
The invariant is fine. Initialization is fine. Maintenance is fine. What changed is `¬B`. The
guard is now a conjunction, so its negation is a *disjunction* — the loop exits when
`i == len(xs)` **or** when `xs[i] > target` — and only the first of those two implies that the
target is nowhere. The `return -1` was justified by an argument that quietly stopped applying the
moment a second way of leaving was added. Baburao's channel, in Python.

The repair is not to delete the optimisation. It is to state the precondition it needs — `xs` is
sorted ascending — and then the disjunct `xs[i] > target` does imply absence, because everything
to the right is at least as large. An optimisation that narrows the guard always narrows what the
exit proves, and if you cannot say what the new exit proves, you have not finished writing it.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation**, for the worst case; **expectation**, for the average — and the two exits
are exactly why this loop has an average case worth computing at all, unlike the oblivious loop in
[1.1](1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md).

Let `n = len(xs)`. The body is constant work `c` per iteration. If the target is found at index
`k`, the body has run `k + 1` times:

```
T(n) = Σ (j = 0 … k) c  =  c(k + 1)
```

For the worst case, `k` is `n - 1` or the target is absent, and the loop runs `n` times: `Θ(n)`.

For the average, state the probability space or the number means nothing. Assume the target is
present, and that its position is uniform over the `n` indices — so `P(k = j) = 1/n` for each `j`.
By linearity of expectation the number of comparisons is

```
E[comparisons] = Σ (j = 1 … n) j · (1/n)  =  (1/n) · n(n + 1)/2  =  (n + 1)/2
```

Half the list, on average, which is `Θ(n)` as well. The constant halved and the class did not
move — and if your inputs are mostly *misses* rather than hits, that distribution is wrong and
every lookup costs the full `n`.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `Θ(1)`, one comparison | `target` at index 0 |
| Average | `(n + 1) / 2` comparisons, `Θ(n)` | Target present, position uniform |
| Worst | `n` comparisons, `Θ(n)` | Target absent, or at the last index |

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary — one integer.

**What the constant hides.** Measured on this machine, CPython 3.12.12, one million integers, the
worst case (absent target), best of five runs:

```
python loop, absent : 84.7 ms
list.index, absent  : 12.5 ms
```

Roughly 6.8×, and the whole of the difference is the interpreter: `list.index` runs the same
comparison sequence in C. Both are `Θ(n)`, both perform exactly `n` comparisons, and one takes
seven times as long — which is Day 1's lesson arriving again from a different direction, and the
reason this curriculum insists you derive the class *and* measure the constant, because neither
one is the answer to "which should I ship".

## 8. When it breaks

The near-miss above fails silently, which is bad enough. The sentinel it returns fails worse,
because Python will happily use it:

```
xs = [4, 7, 2]  xs[index_of(xs, 99)] -> 2
```

`99` is absent, `index_of` returns `-1` to say so, and `xs[-1]` is the **last element of the
list**. No exception, no warning; a lookup that failed comes back as a plausible value and travels
onward through the program. The convention "return `-1` for not found" is borrowed from languages
where `-1` is not a valid index, and in Python every negative integer is a valid index. The
sentinel and the language disagree about what `-1` means, and the disagreement is silent.

Contrast what the standard library does at the same exit:

```
ValueError: 99 is not in list
```

`list.index` refuses to encode "absent" as a value at all. Its exit condition produces a different
*kind* of thing, so no caller can accidentally use it as an index.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is `if idx != -1:` at every call site,
which works until the one call site where somebody forgets. The fix is to make the two exits
produce two different types, so that forgetting is a type error rather than a wrong answer —
`int | None` with `None` for absent, or a raised exception, or returning `(found, index)`. Each
exit of a loop proves a different proposition, and when a function's return type cannot tell
those propositions apart, the compiler and the reader are both being asked to keep the distinction
in their heads. They will not. This is the same reasoning that makes `Optional` types and
`Result` types worth their syntax, and it starts here, on Day 2, as a claim about loop exits.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** The exit condition, not the invariant, is what dominates the cost story.

*Misses are the expensive case, and misses are the common case.* Every hit can exit early;
only an absent key pays the full `n`. So for a lookup structure the interesting latency question
is never "how fast is a hit" — it is "how fast is a miss", because caches, routers, and databases
spend most of their lookups discovering that something is not there. At `n = 10⁹` on disk, a
linear miss is not slow, it is unavailable. The industrial answer is to make the negative exit
cheap without doing the scan: a Bloom filter answers *definitely not present* in constant time and
never answers *present* falsely-negatively, which is `RND-06` on Day 201 and is, exactly, an
engineered `¬B`.

*Early exit destroys predictability.* A loop with a data-dependent exit has a latency distribution
rather than a latency. Tail latency — the p99 — is set by the inputs that leave by the far door,
so a system that looks fine on averages falls over on the customer whose key is absent. Systems
that need predictable timing (cryptographic comparison, most obviously) deliberately give up the
early exit and always run the full loop, because the *time* at which you exit is itself a leak of
which door you took.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "The loop's fine. The `return -1` isn't — `xs[-1]` is a real element in this language and one
> of these call sites is going to index with it. Return `None` and let the type checker do the
> arguing. And on the sorted-input shortcut: if the function requires sorted input, say so in the
> docstring and assert it in debug, because right now the only thing standing between us and a
> wrong answer is that nobody has passed unsorted data yet."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you produce the loop and state the invariant:

1. *"Why is `return -1` at the bottom correct?"* — checking whether you reach for the invariant
   *and* the negated guard, or only re-narrate the loop. One clause is a fail.
2. *"I've added `and xs[i] <= target` to your guard. What does your final `return` prove now?"* —
   checking whether you understand that narrowing a guard widens the set of exit conditions, and
   that the proof must be redone rather than assumed.
3. *"Same function, but return the index of the last occurrence."* — checking whether you can
   restate the invariant rather than patch the code. The early `return` has to go, the invariant
   becomes *the best index seen so far is the largest match in `xs[0:i]`*, and the exit argument
   changes shape with it.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Write the three-part obligation for a `while` loop in symbols, and say which part
   [1.1](1.1-initialization-and-maintenance.md) covered.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `P` holds before the loop; `{P ∧ B} S {P}` for the body; and `P ∧ ¬B ⇒ Q`. 1.1 covered the
   first two — initialization and maintenance. This document is the third, and it is the one that
   actually produces the answer.
   </details>

2. Baburao's line stopped at the channel and the invariant was still perfectly true. In one
   sentence, what was missing?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `¬B` was the wrong sentence. The invariant tells you about the searched region; only "there is
   no unsearched region left" turns that into a statement about the field. Stopping at the channel
   makes `¬B` mean "we reached the water", which implies nothing about the cane beyond it.
   </details>

3. A loop has one `while` guard and two `return`s inside the body. How many exit obligations are
   there, and are they the same obligation?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Three, and they are different. Each door has its own condition `C_e` under which control leaves,
   and each needs `P ∧ C_e ⇒ Q` proved separately. Sharing one line of reasoning across doors that
   leave under different conditions is precisely the near-miss in §6.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** Here is a loop whose invariant is *`largest` is the maximum of `xs[0:i]`*, with
   correct initialization and maintenance. Construct the input that makes it return the wrong
   answer, and say which of the three obligations fails.

   ```python
   i, largest = 1, xs[0]
   while i < len(xs) and xs[i] != 0:
       if xs[i] > largest:
           largest = xs[i]
       i += 1
   return largest
   ```
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `[3, 0, 9]` returns 3; the maximum is 9. Initialization holds and maintenance holds — at every
   top of the loop `largest` really is the maximum of `xs[0:i]`. The third obligation fails: `¬B`
   is now `i == len(xs) ∨ xs[i] == 0`, and the second disjunct leaves a suffix of the list
   unexamined, so the invariant about a *prefix* never becomes a claim about the whole. Same
   disease as the sorted-input shortcut, and note again that the flawed line is the `return`, not
   anything inside the loop.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-01`.** Day 1 distinguished a specification from a procedure. Which of the
   three obligations is the one that mentions the specification, and what does that tell you about
   the order in which to write things?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The exit obligation, `P ∧ ¬B ⇒ Q` — `Q` *is* the specification, the statement of what must be
   true at the end. The other two obligations never mention it. So the postcondition has to exist
   before the proof can be finished, which means writing the specification down first is not
   ceremony: without `Q` there is literally nothing to prove the loop against, and "it looks
   right" is what fills the vacuum.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** §7 says both the Python loop and `list.index` perform exactly `n`
   comparisons on a miss, and one is 6.8× slower. Which assumption of the RAM model is doing the
   lying, and is the model wrong?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The unit-cost assumption: the model charges 1 for a step whose cost does not grow with `n`, and
   both a bytecode-dispatched comparison and a C-level comparison qualify — they differ by a
   constant, and the model deliberately discards constants. The model is not wrong, it is
   answering a different question. It predicts that doubling `n` doubles both times, and it does.
   It says nothing about which to ship, and was never supposed to.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A colleague changes a loop guard from `while lo < hi` to `while lo < hi and not
   found` and the tests still pass. What do you ask for in review?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The updated argument for whatever the code does after the loop. The guard now has two ways to
   go false, so the post-loop code faces a disjunction where it used to face one condition, and
   any line that assumed `lo == hi` is now unjustified on one branch. Passing tests are not
   evidence here — the new exit is reachable only on inputs the suite may not contain, which is
   exactly the shape of the `[9, 3]` case in §6.
   </details>
