# 1.1 — Initialization and maintenance

## 1. The one-line answer

A loop invariant is a claim about the loop's own state that you make true before the first
iteration and that every iteration leaves true, so nobody ever has to check it again.

---

## 2. The story

It is a quarter past six in the morning and the counting hall in Nashik has been awake all night.

Sunita Bhosale is the returning officer for the ward. Running the length of the hall, stuck to
the floor, is a line of red tape. On the near side of it: forty-one steel trays of ballot papers
on trolleys, still to be counted. On the far side: the trays that are finished. Behind her,
chalked on a slate the size of a door, is a single number.

There is a rule in this hall, and everyone in it knows it — the counting assistants, the three
party agents in plastic chairs, the constable at the door. **The number on the slate is exactly
the number of votes in the trays on the far side of the tape.** Not roughly. Not once the shift
ends. Exactly, at every moment.

That rule is expensive to keep. A tray does not cross the tape and then get added to the slate a
little later, when someone has a free hand. The assistant carries the tray across, calls the
count out loud, and the chalk changes in the same movement, with an agent from each party
watching both the tray and the slate. It is slower. It looks like ceremony. It is not.

At twenty to five an agent stood up and demanded the count stop. He had seen something he did
not like. Everything froze — trolleys where they stood, hands off the trays. And in that frozen
room the slate was still true. They could check it: count the far side, compare. There had been
no moment in the night when the slate was a number that had not caught up yet. The objection was
recorded and dismissed, and the hall started again from exactly where it had stopped.

Sunita has worked a count where the rule was not kept. Someone moved four trays and updated the
slate afterwards, in one go, because it was faster. An objection landed in the gap. The slate
said one thing and the far side of the tape said another, and there was no way, at three in the
morning, with everybody exhausted and nobody agreeing, to establish which of the two was wrong.
They counted the whole ward again.

Now the near side is empty. The last trolley has crossed. Nobody has to add anything up, or
reconcile anything, or check anything — the number on the slate has been the answer to the
question all night. It only becomes *interesting* at this exact moment, because there is finally
nothing left on the near side for it to be missing.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

The slate is a **loop invariant**. The red tape is the boundary between the work that is done and
the work that is not. One tray crossing the tape is one **iteration**.

The rule of the hall has two halves, and they are proved separately.

**Initialization.** Before the first tray moved, the far side of the tape was empty and the slate
read zero. Zero votes on the far side, zero on the slate: the claim was true before anything
happened. This half is usually easy and it is usually the half that is skipped, which is a
mistake — the near-miss in §6 is a program whose *only* fault is that this half is false.

**Maintenance.** If the claim is true when a tray starts to cross, it is true again once the
crossing is complete and the chalk has changed. Not true *during* — for a moment, mid-carry, the
tray is over the line and the slate has not moved, and in that moment the claim is false. Nobody
minds. The obligation is at the boundaries between iterations, not at every instant inside one.

Put those two together and you have proof by induction over iterations, which is the only thing
happening here: true at the start, and each step preserves it, therefore true after every step
anyone cares to take. You never have to re-examine the far side of the tape. That is the entire
economic argument for invariants — a claim you pay for once, that then holds for free, at every
stopping point, forever.

Notice what an invariant is *not*. It is not a comment describing what the code does. It is not a
fact you observed to be true while stepping through a debugger. It is an obligation you take on,
and the body of the loop is written to discharge it.

> **Formally:** let `P` be a predicate over the loop's state. If `P` holds immediately before the
> first iteration (initialization), and if `P` holding at the top of an arbitrary iteration
> implies `P` holds at the top of the following iteration (maintenance), then by induction `P`
> holds at the top of every iteration and immediately after the loop's last one.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **CPython's `list.sort()` — Timsort's run stack.** Timsort splits the input into ascending runs
  and pushes them onto a stack, keeping invariants over the lengths of the topmost runs: each
  must exceed the sum of the two above it. Those invariants are what bound the stack's depth, and
  the stack is sized from that bound. In 2015 a group at CWI Amsterdam and Radboud University
  tried to *prove* the invariants mechanically and found they are not in fact maintained — the
  merging rule only checked part of the condition. The same algorithm in Java's
  `java.util.Collections.sort` could be driven into an `ArrayIndexOutOfBoundsException` on inputs
  of roughly sixty-seven million elements. The bug sat in shipping code for years, in one of the
  most-executed sorting routines on earth, and what found it was somebody writing the invariant
  down formally and asking whether the code preserves it.

- **SQLite's B-tree, and the `assert()` statements it ships with.** SQLite's tree code is dense
  with `assert()` calls that state invariants — this page is not overfull, this cursor points at a
  valid cell, this key is greater than the previous one. Those assertions are compiled out of the
  release build with `NDEBUG`, so they cost users nothing; they exist to be *on* during SQLite's
  own test runs, where they turn "the database is subtly corrupted" into "the process died at the
  exact line where the claim first stopped being true". That is an invariant used as a tripwire
  rather than as a proof, and both uses are legitimate.

- **Asked as:** *"Walk me through why your solution is correct."* Most candidates answer by
  re-executing their own code aloud on a small example — "so `i` is zero here, then it becomes
  one…". That is a trace, not an argument; it says nothing about the input they did not pick. What
  is being asked for is one sentence about what the loop keeps true, and then two more about why
  it starts true and why the body preserves it.

## 5. The mechanism

Take the simplest loop that has anything to prove: add up a list of integers.

```
                                    i = 3
                                      |
                                      v
    index       0     1     2     3     4     5
             +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
    xs       |  4  |  7  |  2  |  9  |  1  |  5  |
             +-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+
              \_______________/ \_________________/
               far side of the   near side of the tape:
               tape: already     not yet added
               added
               total == 13
```

*Notice what the picture makes obvious and the code does not: the invariant is a statement about
`total` **and** `i` together. Neither variable alone can carry it. `total == 13` is not a claim
anyone can check — thirteen of what? The claim is that `total` is the sum of everything left of
`i`, and the moment you write that down you can see that any line touching one of the two
variables had better touch the other.*

**The invariant:** *`total` is the sum of `xs[0:i]` — every element to the left of `i`, and no
element at or to the right of it.*

### A trace

Input `[4, 7, 2]`. State recorded at the **top** of each iteration, which is where the obligation
lives.

| At the top of | `i` | `total` | `xs[0:i]` | Invariant holds? |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| iteration 1 | 0 | 0 | `[]` | 0 == sum of nothing ✓ |
| iteration 2 | 1 | 4 | `[4]` | 4 == 4 ✓ |
| iteration 3 | 2 | 11 | `[4, 7]` | 11 == 11 ✓ |
| after the loop | 3 | 13 | `[4, 7, 2]` | 13 == 13 ✓ |

The first row is initialization, and it is the row that looks like nothing. `xs[0:0]` is the empty
list, the sum of no numbers is zero, and `total` is zero. It is true *vacuously* — because there
is nothing for it to be false about. That is not a loophole. A claim about an empty collection
holding for free is exactly what lets the induction start.

## 6. Line by line

```python
def total_of(xs: list[int]) -> int:
    total = 0
    i = 0
```

Two variables, and the invariant is about both. `total = 0` is not "an obvious starting value" —
it is the value that makes the claim true before any work happens. If you cannot say which
initialisation makes your invariant true, you do not yet know your invariant.

Why an explicit `i` and a `while`, rather than `for x in xs`? Because the boundary needs a name.
`for x in xs` is the better code and you should write it once you trust the loop, but it hides the
tape, and while you are proving something you want the tape visible. The proof is easier to write
about a slightly clumsier program, and that is a trade you make deliberately.

```python
    while i < len(xs):
        total += xs[i]
```

Here is the part that surprises people the first time. Immediately after this line the invariant
is **false**: `total` now includes `xs[i]`, and `i` still says it does not. That is fine. It is the
tray in mid-air, over the line, before the chalk moves. The obligation is at the top of each
iteration, not after every statement — so an invariant that is briefly untrue inside the body is
not a defect, and someone who inserts an assertion into the middle of the body and watches it fail
has misread the contract rather than found a bug.

```python
        i += 1
```

This is the chalk. It is not bookkeeping and it is not "advancing the loop" — it is the line that
repairs the claim the previous line broke. Maintenance is exactly this: by the time control
returns to the top, the body has put the world back into a state where the claim holds again.

```python
    return total
```

The loop has stopped and the invariant still holds. Turning "the invariant still holds" into "and
therefore this function is correct" is a separate step with its own way of going wrong, and it is
what [1.2](1.2-exit-and-negated-guard.md) is about.

**The near-miss.** Here is a version people write, and it is wrong in a way that reads as
experience rather than as carelessness:

```python
def total_of_seeded(xs: list[int]) -> int:
    total = xs[0]          # seed with the first element, then walk the rest
    for x in xs:           # ... except this walks all of them
        total += x
    return total
```

Seeding an accumulator with `xs[0]` is a good habit imported from the wrong place. It is correct
for a maximum — Day 1's `best = xs[0]` works precisely because the loop then walks the
*remaining* elements. Here the seed says the tape starts after element zero, and the loop starts
it at element zero. The two halves of the proof disagree about where the boundary is, and nothing
in the code says so out loud.

```
>>> total_of_seeded([4, 7])
15
>>> total_of_seeded([5])
10
>>> total_of_seeded([2, 3, 4])
11
```

`[2, 3, 4]` gives 11 instead of 9, `[4, 7]` gives 15 instead of 11, `[5]` gives 10 instead of 5 —
always wrong, always by exactly `xs[0]`, never loudly. And notice the shape of it: an
initialization that establishes `total == sum(xs[0:1])` bolted to a loop whose maintenance step
assumes `total == sum(xs[0:i])` starting from `i = 0`. Both halves are individually reasonable.
Only the pair is wrong, which is why reading the loop body carefully will never find this bug.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation.**

Let `n = len(xs)`. The body performs a fixed amount of work per iteration — one index, one
addition into `total`, one addition into `i`, one comparison for the guard. Call it `c`. The guard
is true for `i = 0, 1, …, n - 1` and false at `i = n`, so the body runs `n` times:

```
T(n) = Σ (i = 0 … n-1) c  =  c · n  =  Θ(n)
```

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `Θ(n)` | Any list of length `n` |
| Average | `Θ(n)` | Any list of length `n` |
| Worst | `Θ(n)` | Any list of length `n` |

All three rows are identical, and that is worth naming rather than skipping past. This loop has no
early exit and no data-dependent branch, so its operation count is a function of `n` alone — the
same property Day 1's comparison counter exposed, and the word for it is **oblivious**. For an
oblivious procedure "best case" and "worst case" carry no information, and an interviewer asking
for them is checking whether you will invent a distinction that is not there.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary — two integers, whatever `n` is. The list itself is input and does not
count. (Auxiliary versus total is `CPX-12`, Day 8.)

**What the constant hides.** Measured on this machine, CPython 3.12.12, one million integers, best
of five runs:

```
python loop :  39.4   ms
builtin sum :  11.075 ms
```

About 3.6× — a much narrower gap than Day 1's palindrome loop, which lost to a slice by 34×, and
the reason is worth having. `sum()` runs its loop in C, so the per-iteration interpreter dispatch
disappears. But it is still adding *Python integer objects* one at a time, which means a type
check and possibly an allocation per element, and none of that is avoidable while the elements are
`int` objects in a `list`. The C loop wins where it is allowed to win and no further. When a
library function is three times faster rather than thirty, that ratio is telling you which part of
the work was interpreter overhead and which part was the actual job.

### The second cost, which is the one Day 2 is really about

Checking an invariant is not free. Write the claim out as an assertion:

```python
        assert total == sum(xs[:i])
```

At the top of iteration `i` that assertion copies `i` elements and adds them up. Summing over the
whole loop:

```
Σ (i = 0 … n-1) i  =  n(n - 1) / 2  =  Θ(n²)
```

A `Θ(n)` loop with its invariant checked at every step is a `Θ(n²)` program. Measured, same
machine, best of two:

```
checked n= 1000:     4.35 ms   ratio    -
checked n= 2000:    18.81 ms   ratio 4.32
checked n= 4000:    94.67 ms   ratio 5.03
checked n= 8000:   394.17 ms   ratio 4.16
```

Ratios near 4, exactly as `n(n-1)/2` predicts when `n` doubles. This is not an argument against
checking invariants — it is the reason today's `lab/` checks yours on small inputs only, the
reason `python -O` strips `assert` statements, and the reason SQLite compiles its assertions out of
release builds. **A checked loop is a different algorithm from the loop it checks**, and if you
forget that you will benchmark the wrong program.

## 8. When it breaks

Maintenance is where the loud failures live. Move `i += 1` above the accumulation — a very
ordinary slip, and it even reads better, since "step, then use" is how people describe walking —
and you get this:

```
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "d2a.py", line 17, in <module>
    total_stepped([4, 7])
  File "d2a.py", line 12, in total_stepped
    total += xs[i]
             ~~^^^
IndexError: list index out of range
```

CPython 3.11 and later underline the exact subexpression that raised. The `~~^^^` sits under
`xs[i]`, not under `total`, so before thinking about anything you already know the fault is in the
index and not in the accumulator.

What actually went wrong is not "an off-by-one". It is that the body no longer restores the
invariant. With `i += 1` running first, the code adds `xs[i]` — an element now on the *far* side of
the tape, one it has already stepped past — so at the top of the next iteration `total` is the sum
of some elements that are not `xs[0:i]` at all. The claim was abandoned on the first iteration; the
`IndexError` is merely where the wreckage became visible, `n` iterations later.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** Swapping the two lines back is the patch. The fix is
an order of operations you adopt for the rest of the curriculum: **write the invariant above the
loop as a comment before you write the body, then read each line of the body against it.** With
`# invariant: total == sum(xs[:i])` sitting on the screen, "add `xs[i]`, then move `i` past it" is
the only order that agrees with the sentence. The alternative — shuffling `i += 1` up and down
until the tests pass — produces code that is correct by coincidence, and the coincidence does not
survive the next edit. Phase 7 is seven days of binary search, every bug in it is a maintenance bug
of exactly this shape, and the people who find those bugs quickly are the ones who wrote the
sentence down first.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** Three things change, and only the first is about size.

*The check stops being affordable.* At `n = 10⁹` you cannot verify `total == sum(xs[:i])` on every
iteration; you would be running a quadratic program over a billion elements. Real systems degrade
the check rather than delete it — verify at checkpoints instead of every step, verify a sampled
one-in-a-million iterations, or verify a cheap consequence of the invariant instead of the
invariant itself (a running checksum rather than a full re-sum). What you must not do is quietly
stop believing the claim because you stopped checking it.

*Concurrency destroys single-threaded invariants for free.* The slate works because one hand moves
the chalk. Put two counting teams in the hall, both reading the slate, both adding their own tray,
and the updates interleave: both read four hundred, both write their own four hundred plus a tray,
and one tray's votes vanish. Nothing about the loop body changed — the invariant was always a claim
about a *sequence* of states, and concurrent execution stops there being a sequence. The repair is
to make the read-and-update indivisible: a lock, or a compare-and-swap that fails and retries when
somebody else moved the chalk first. This is `CNC-02` on Day 195, and it is why "my loop is
correct" and "my loop is correct when four threads run it" are unrelated claims.

*Assertions are not a safety net in the build that matters.* `python -O` removes every `assert`
statement from the bytecode; C compiled with `-DNDEBUG` removes every `assert()`. So an assertion
that does real work — mutating state, or validating untrusted input — silently stops happening in
exactly the build your users run. Invariant checks go in assertions; input validation raises real
exceptions.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "This is right, but the only person who knows why is you, and in four months that won't be you
> either. Put the invariant on one line above the `while` — what's true about `total` and `i`
> together — and the next person can check the body against it without re-deriving your reasoning
> from scratch. Also, that `assert` inside the loop re-sums the prefix every iteration; it's
> quadratic and it's in the shipping path. Move it behind a debug flag or delete it."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you state the invariant correctly:

1. *"Is your invariant true in the middle of the loop body?"* — checking whether you understand
   that the obligation is at iteration boundaries. Answering "yes, always" is the wrong answer, and
   it usually means the invariant was memorised rather than derived.
2. *"Your loop is `Θ(n)`. What is it if I assert the invariant on every iteration?"* — checking
   that you can cost the check as its own program, and that you noticed it is not the same program.
3. *"Now suppose two threads run this loop over disjoint halves and add into the same `total`.
   Which of your two proof obligations fails?"* — checking whether you can locate the break.
   Neither fails in isolation; what fails is the premise that states form a sequence at all.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. State the two obligations by name, and say which one is usually skipped.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   **Initialization** — the claim is true before the first iteration — and **maintenance** — if the
   claim is true at the top of an iteration, it is true at the top of the next. Initialization is
   the one that gets skipped, because it usually holds vacuously and looks like it is not saying
   anything. The `total_of_seeded` near-miss is a program whose maintenance step is flawless and
   whose initialization is false.
   </details>

2. Why is "`total == 13`" not a loop invariant?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because it is not a claim you can evaluate at an arbitrary point in the loop and get a
   meaningful answer from. An invariant relates the accumulated result to *how far the loop has
   got*, so it must mention the boundary. `total == sum(xs[:i])` mentions both variables and is
   checkable at any iteration; `total == 13` is true at exactly one moment and says nothing about
   any other.
   </details>

3. Someone puts `assert total == sum(xs[:i])` between the two statements in the body and reports
   that your invariant "doesn't hold". Are they right?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   No, and the mistake is worth naming precisely. The invariant is an obligation at the top of each
   iteration. Between `total += xs[i]` and `i += 1` the body is mid-repair — the tray is over the
   line and the chalk has not moved. Placed at the top of the loop, the same assertion passes every
   time. The contract is about the boundaries between iterations, not about every instruction
   inside one.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** Here is a loop with a perfectly good invariant, `total == sum(xs[:i])`, and a
   perfectly good initialization. Construct an input on which it returns the wrong answer anyway:

   ```python
   i, total = 0, 0
   while i < len(xs) - 1:
       total += xs[i]
       i += 1
   ```
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Any non-empty list: `[4, 7]` returns 4, `[5]` returns 0. The invariant is true at every
   iteration and true at exit — `total` really is the sum of `xs[:i]`. The invariant is simply not
   *enough*, because the loop stops with `i == n - 1` instead of `i == n`, and nothing in the claim
   says otherwise. Everything in this document can be discharged perfectly and still leave you with
   a wrong program: that gap is exactly what [1.2](1.2-exit-and-negated-guard.md) exists to close.
   Note also `[]`, where `len(xs) - 1` is `-1`, the guard is false immediately, and it returns 0 —
   the right answer for none of the right reasons.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-01`.** Day 1 said correctness is *not* one of the five properties of an
   algorithm, but a separate claim requiring separate proof. Which of the five does an invariant
   have nothing to say about, and what does that imply about a loop you have just proved correct?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   **Finiteness.** Initialization and maintenance say what is true *if* control reaches the top of
   an iteration; neither says control ever reaches the last one. A loop that spins forever
   satisfies both obligations vacuously and for eternity. So the proof you have after 1.1 and 1.2
   is worth nothing until it is paired with the argument in
   [2.1](../02-termination-and-measure/2.1-measure-that-decreases.md), and the pair of claims has a
   name — [2.2](../02-termination-and-measure/2.2-partial-versus-total-correctness.md).
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** §7 measured the plain loop at 39.4 ms and the invariant-checked loop at
   394 ms on a list a hundred and twenty-five times smaller. Explain the comparison in terms of the
   RAM model rather than in terms of Python.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   They are different algorithms with different operation counts, and the model prices them
   correctly: `Θ(n)` against `Θ(n²)`. `sum(xs[:i])` looks like one step in the source text and is
   not one step in the model — it copies `i` cells and performs `i` additions, and a step may be
   charged 1 only if its cost does not grow with `n`. This is the same trap as Day 1's "which of
   these is one operation?", wearing an assertion as a disguise.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A colleague's pull request adds `# invariant: the array is sorted` above a loop
   that inserts into a sorted array. What is the one question you ask?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   "Sorted *when*?" The array is not sorted in the middle of an insertion — that is the entire
   period during which elements are being shifted. A useful invariant names the boundary and scopes
   the claim to one side of it: *`xs[0:i]` is sorted, and `xs[i:]` is untouched*. An invariant that
   is false at the moment anyone would want to rely on it is a comment, not an obligation.
   </details>
