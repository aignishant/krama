# 2.1 — Big-O and its witness

## 1. The one-line answer

Writing `f(n) = O(g(n))` is making a promise, and the promise is proved by producing two numbers: a
multiplier, and a size beyond which `f` never exceeds that multiplier times `g`.

---

## 2. The story

The negotiation has been going for two hours in a room above the weaving shed in Coimbatore, and
the tea has been sent for twice.

Meenakshi Sundaram runs transport. Across the table is the purchase manager of a mill that moves
about four hundred tonnes of yarn a month, and who wants one thing in the contract that Meenakshi
will not put in it: a delivery time in hours.

"Chennai in eighteen hours," the manager says. "Write it."

Meenakshi will not write it, and the reasons are all outside the room: a lorry strike, a bandh, a
washed-out culvert near Salem, a checkpost queue at four in the morning. Promise a number of hours
and you are promising the roads will behave, which nobody can promise and everybody has been sued
over.

What Meenakshi offers instead sounds, at first, like a refusal dressed up. "I will guarantee this.
On any route, for any consignment above two tonnes, we will not take more than three times what the
railway takes on the same route. In writing. Three times. If the railway does it in a day, we are
inside three days, always."

The manager objects immediately, and the objection is a good one: what about the small stuff? A
fifty kilo parcel goes by railway in a day and takes Meenakshi four, because loading a fifty kilo
parcel takes exactly as long as loading two tonnes of it — the paperwork, the weighbridge, the
waiting for the lorry to fill. Below two tonnes the fixed costs swamp the whole journey and no
multiple holds at all.

"Correct," says Meenakshi. "That is why the promise starts at two tonnes. Below that I make no
promise and you should not accept one."

So the guarantee has exactly two numbers in it, and both had to be argued over. **Three** — the
multiplier. **Two tonnes** — where the promise begins.

The manager's clerk spends the next week trying to break it, which is the correct response to any
guarantee. He goes through eighteen months of consignment notes looking for a single load above two
tonnes where the lorry took more than three times the train. One would be enough. He does not find
one, and the contract gets signed with those two numbers written into clause nine.

Two years later there is a dispute, and it is settled in an afternoon rather than in court, because
the clause does not require anybody to agree about what the roads are like. It requires one weight,
one train time, one lorry time, and a multiplication.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

The transport guarantee is a big-O claim, and it has the same three moving parts.

The **function being bounded** is the lorry's time. The **function bounding it** is the railway's
time. The **multiplier**, three, is the constant. And the **starting size**, two tonnes, is the
threshold below which the claim is silent — silent, not false, and for the reason the manager gave:
small inputs are dominated by fixed costs that no multiple of the comparison function can cover.

So: `f(n) = O(g(n))` means there is a constant `c` and a size `n₀` such that `f(n) ≤ c · g(n)` for
every `n ≥ n₀`. The pair `(c, n₀)` is called a **witness**, and the word is exact — to prove the
claim you produce them, the way Meenakshi produced three and two tonnes. Nothing else is required
and nothing else is accepted.

Three consequences that follow immediately, all of which people get wrong:

**Witnesses are not unique, and no particular one is special.** If `(3, 2)` works then `(4, 2)`
works, and `(3, 5)` works, and `(100, 1000)` works. Making `c` bigger or `n₀` bigger can never break
a claim that already holds. So there is no point hunting for "the right constant" — any pair proves
it, and the smallest pair proves nothing extra.

**Disproving is a different shape of argument.** To kill `f = O(g)` you cannot exhibit a
counterexample the way the clerk was looking for one, because a single bad consignment could always
be excused by raising `c`. You have to show that *no* pair works: for every `c` and every `n₀` there
is some `n ≥ n₀` with `f(n) > c · g(n)`. In practice that means showing the ratio `f(n)/g(n)` grows
without bound.

**The equals sign is a lie of convention.** `f(n) = O(g(n))` is read "f is *in* O(g)", where `O(g)`
is the set of all functions bounded above by a constant multiple of `g` beyond some point. The
equality does not go backwards: `n = O(n²)` is true and `O(n²) = n` is meaningless. Everybody writes
it with `=` and everybody means `∈`, which is the sort of thing that only confuses people who are
paying attention — so pay attention once, here, and then write it the way everyone else does.

> **Formally:** `f(n) = O(g(n))` iff there exist constants `c > 0` and `n₀ ∈ ℕ` such that
> `0 ≤ f(n) ≤ c · g(n)` for all `n ≥ n₀`. The pair `(c, n₀)` is a witness to the claim; exhibiting
> one is a proof, and no other form of proof is needed.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **The C++ standard, where complexity is normative text.** The standard does not merely describe
  what `std::sort` computes; it *requires* `O(N log N)` comparisons, and it requires
  `std::vector::push_back` to be amortized constant, and `std::map::find` to be `O(log n)`. An
  implementation that sorts correctly in quadratic time is non-conforming — as wrong as one that
  returns the wrong order. This is the notation used as a contract between a standards committee and
  the people who write libraries, and it is why the language's containers can be reasoned about
  without reading their source. It is also why `std::list::size` became `O(1)` in C++11: the
  committee changed the required complexity, and implementations had to add a counter.

- **Redis, where every command's documentation names its complexity.** `GET` is `O(1)`. `LRANGE` is
  `O(S+N)`. `SMEMBERS` is `O(N)`. `KEYS` is `O(N)` over the whole keyspace, and its documentation
  says in as many words not to use it in production. The reason those labels are on the page is that
  Redis executes commands one at a time on a single thread: an `O(N)` command against a large
  collection does not slow itself down, it stops the entire server for every other client. Engineers
  read those complexity classes daily, as an operational instruction rather than as theory.

- **Asked as:** *"Show that `3n² + 7n + 2` is `O(n²)`."* — usually early, usually as a filter. The
  expected answer is not a paragraph about dominant terms; it is two numbers and one line of
  arithmetic. §5 does it twice.

## 5. The mechanism

The picture the definition is describing:

```
    cost
      ^
      |                                                    c·g(n)
      |                                              .  '
      |                                     .  '        f(n)
      |                            .  '          _____ -
      |                   .  '        _____ -
      |          .  '      ___ -
      |    . '  ___ -
      |  __-
      |-'
      +--------|---------------------------------------------->  n
               n₀
        <------->|<--------------------------------------------
        no claim |   c·g(n) is above f(n) here, and stays above
        is made  |
```

*What to notice: to the left of `n₀` the two curves cross freely and the definition says nothing
about it. To the right, `c·g` is a ceiling. The claim is not that `f` is close to `c·g`, or that it
approaches it — only that it never gets above it. A ceiling is all `O` ever asserts, and
[2.2](2.2-omega-theta-and-the-abuse-of-o.md) is about how much less that is than people hear.*

**The invariant:** *beyond `n₀`, the curve `c·g` never goes below `f` — for every single `n`, not on
average and not in the limit.*

### Two traces of the same claim

**Claim:** `3n² + 7n + 2 = O(n²)`.

*Witness one, chosen for ease.* Take `n₀ = 1`. For every `n ≥ 1` we have `n ≤ n²` and `1 ≤ n²`, so:

```
3n² + 7n + 2  ≤  3n² + 7n² + 2n²  =  12n²        for all n ≥ 1
```

Witness `(c, n₀) = (12, 1)`. Done — that is a complete proof, and the trick in it is the only trick
you need: replace every lower-order term by the dominant one, which can only make the left side
bigger.

*Witness two, chosen to be tight.* Suppose you want `c = 4`. Then you need `3n² + 7n + 2 ≤ 4n²`,
that is `7n + 2 ≤ n²`, which holds from `n = 8` upwards:

| `n` | `3n² + 7n + 2` | `4n²` | holds? |
|---:|---:|---:|:--|
| 6 | 152 | 144 | no |
| 7 | 198 | 196 | no |
| 8 | 250 | 256 | **yes** |
| 9 | 308 | 324 | yes |
| 100 | 30 702 | 40 000 | yes |

Witness `(4, 8)`. Both witnesses prove exactly the same claim. The second is not better — it is
merely tighter, and tightness of the constant is not a property the notation records. Notice how the
two numbers trade against each other: accept a bigger `c` and you can start at `n₀ = 1`; insist on a
small `c` and the promise has to start later. That is the same trade Meenakshi and the purchase
manager were making across the table.

## 6. Line by line

You cannot check an asymptotic claim by running it — the claim is about infinitely many `n`. What
you *can* do is compute the smallest multiplier that works over a finite range, which is a useful
instrument as long as you remember what it is not.

```python
from collections.abc import Callable

def smallest_c(f: Callable[[int], float], g: Callable[[int], float],
               n0: int, limit: int) -> float:
    """The smallest c making f(n) <= c*g(n) hold for every n in [n0, limit]."""
    return max(f(n) / g(n) for n in range(n0, limit + 1))
```

One line, and it is worth seeing why the maximum of the ratio is the answer. The condition
`f(n) ≤ c · g(n)` rearranges (for positive `g`) to `c ≥ f(n)/g(n)`. It has to hold at *every* `n` in
the range, so `c` must be at least the largest of those ratios — and that largest ratio works, since
it dominates all the others. The smallest valid `c` is exactly the peak of the ratio curve.

```python
def first_violation(f, g, c: float, n0: int, limit: int) -> int | None:
    """The smallest n in [n0, limit] where the promise fails, or None if it holds."""
    for n in range(n0, limit + 1):
        if f(n) > c * g(n):
            return n
    return None
```

This is the clerk with the eighteen months of consignment notes: it looks for one counterexample and
reports where. Note what it returns on success — `None`, meaning "no violation *in this range*" —
and how carefully that is not the same as "the claim holds". Naming the function `first_violation`
rather than `check_bound` is deliberate: a function that reports violations cannot be misread as a
function that certifies claims, and the name is the only defence you get.

```python
    return None
```

The most dangerous line in this document. Everything that goes wrong in §8 goes wrong by treating
this `None` as a proof.

**The near-miss.** Here is the tool used the way it is always used first — to "verify" an asymptotic
claim empirically:

```python
def verify(f, g, limit: int = 1000) -> float:
    c = smallest_c(f, g, 1, limit)
    print(f"f(n) <= {c} * g(n) for all n -- verified up to {limit}")
    return c
```

Run it on `f(n) = n²` and `g(n) = 1000n`:

```
checked up to n =     100:  smallest c that works = 0.100
checked up to n =     500:  smallest c that works = 0.500
checked up to n =    1000:  smallest c that works = 1.000
checked up to n =    5000:  smallest c that works = 5.000
checked up to n =  100000:  smallest c that works = 100.000
```

The "verified" constant is not a constant. It grows in exact proportion to the range checked,
because `n²/1000n` is `n/1000`, and a function whose ratio grows without bound is not `O` of the
other one at all. The input that kills the near-miss is simply *a larger limit*, which is the one
input an empirical check can never try. Any pair of functions can be made to look bounded by
choosing a range small enough — that is what makes this the most seductive wrong argument in the
subject.

## 7. The cost, derived

Two costs are worth separating here: what the check costs, and why the check can never be the proof.

**Technique: summation.**

`smallest_c` evaluates `f`, evaluates `g`, divides, and compares once per value of `n` in the range.
Writing `c_step` for that fixed work and `m = limit - n₀ + 1` for the number of values:

```
T(m) = Σ (i = 1 … m) c_step  =  c_step · m  =  Θ(limit - n₀)
```

Linear in the width of the range, and — the point — **independent of how large the numbers get**,
which is exactly the trap. Doubling `limit` doubles the cost and buys no more certainty than before,
because the set of `n` you have *not* checked is infinite either way. There is no amount of compute
that turns this loop into a proof; the proof is the four lines of algebra in §5, and it covers every
`n` at once because it never mentions a particular one.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `Θ(1)` for `first_violation` | The claim fails at `n₀` — the first value checked |
| Average | `Θ(limit - n₀)` | A claim that holds; every value must be examined |
| Worst | `Θ(limit - n₀)` | Same — a true claim is the expensive case, which is worth noticing |

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary. `max(...)` over a generator holds one running maximum; writing
`max([f(n)/g(n) for n in range(...)])` with a list comprehension instead would build the whole list
and make it `Θ(limit)` — a real `Θ(1)`-to-`Θ(n)` space regression that one bracket introduces, and
one worth having in your fingers.

**What the constant hides.** `f(n)/g(n)` in Python is a float division of two arbitrary-precision
integers, and once the values exceed 2⁵³ the quotient is no longer exact. On `f(n) = n²` at
`n = 10⁸` you are dividing a 10¹⁶ by a 10¹¹ and rounding — so a check that reports "holds by a
margin of one part in 10¹⁷" is reporting rounding noise. Comparing `f(n)` against `c * g(n)` with
integer arithmetic (or `Fraction`) instead of dividing avoids it. This is `CPX-02`'s
arbitrary-precision leak arriving in the tooling built to study `CPX-05`.

## 8. When it breaks

The near-miss in §6 said `c = 1` works. Here is that promise tested one step past the range that
was checked:

```
the claim 'n^2 is O(1000n) with c = 1' tested past the range that was checked:
  n =   1000   f(n) =    1,000,000   1 * g(n) =    1,000,000   holds
  n =   1001   f(n) =    1,002,001   1 * g(n) =    1,001,000   FAILS
  n =   2000   f(n) =    4,000,000   1 * g(n) =    2,000,000   FAILS
  n =  10000   f(n) =  100,000,000   1 * g(n) =   10,000,000   FAILS
```

One step. The check ran to a thousand and passed; at a thousand and one the promise is broken, and by
ten thousand it is broken by a factor of ten and still widening.

This is not a contrived example — it is the shape of the most expensive class of performance
incident there is. Someone benchmarks a component up to the size they have, sees a comfortable
straight line, and writes "linear" in the design document. The claim was never linear; it was
quadratic with a coefficient small enough that the quadratic term had not yet taken over inside the
range tested. Eight months later the table has ten times the rows and the component is a hundred
times slower, and everybody involved can point at a graph that said it was fine.

There is a second, quieter failure in the same code, and it is worth naming because it does not look
like a bug at all:

```
checked up to n =    1001:  smallest c that works = 1.001
```

The tool has silently increased the constant so the claim keeps holding. Every time you re-run a
finite check with a bigger range and it "still passes with a slightly larger `c`", the growing `c` is
the disproof — a genuine `O` relationship has a constant that stops growing.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is to check further, and it fails for the same
reason at the new limit. The fix is to change what kind of claim you are making with what kind of
evidence:

- **Derive the bound algebraically**, as in §5. A witness pair proved on paper covers all `n`.
- **Use the measurement to check the derivation, not to replace it.** The doubling table of
  [1.1](1.1-seconds-do-not-travel.md) is the right instrument, and the right question of it is "does
  the ratio column match what I derived", not "what is the ratio column".
- **Watch the constant rather than the fit.** If `smallest_c` grows as you extend the range, you have
  disproved the claim regardless of how good any individual fit looked.

The general principle, which will recur in every phase of this curriculum: *a finite experiment can
refute an asymptotic claim and can never establish one.* Refutation is cheap and one counterexample
is enough — which is why running the code is still worth doing.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** The `n₀` in the definition is not a technicality; it is where most of the industrial
arguments actually live.

*Bounds that only bite past your size are free, and bounds that bite below it are the problem.*
A structure that is `O(log n)` with `n₀` in the thousands is irrelevant if your `n` is forty — you
are living entirely in the region the guarantee does not describe, exactly like Meenakshi's fifty
kilo parcel. Conversely, a hash table's `O(1)` average lookup has a small `n₀` and a large hidden
constant, which is why an array scan beats a dictionary for a handful of keys. Knowing the definition
tells you the right question: where does this guarantee start, and am I above it?

*The constant that the notation drops is exactly what an SLA cannot drop.* An operations team's
version of a witness pair is a percentile and a threshold — "p99 under 200 ms for payloads above
1 KB" — and it has the same shape for the same reason: a promise with a multiplier and a region of
validity, because promising something about every input including the pathological ones is a promise
nobody can keep. When you write an SLO you are writing a witness, and the arguments about it are
arguments about `c` and `n₀`.

*Documented complexity is an operational hazard warning.* Redis's `KEYS` is `O(N)` and its docs tell
you not to run it; MongoDB's unindexed queries are `O(N)`; a `LIKE '%foo%'` in SQL is a scan. In each
case the complexity label is the thing that lets an engineer predict, before shipping, that the
operation will be fine in staging with ten thousand rows and will stop the world with ten million.
Reading complexity labels as operational warnings rather than as theory is one of the more valuable
habits this phase can leave you with.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "The comment says this is O(n). Over what, and from where? It's linear in the number of rows and
> quadratic in the number of columns, and we have wide tables, so the interesting variable isn't the
> one you named. Also, 'verified by benchmark up to 10k' isn't a bound — extend it to 100k and if
> the fitted constant moves, you've disproved your own claim rather than confirmed it."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you assert a bound:

1. *"Prove it. Give me `c` and `n₀`."* — checking whether you know the definition operationally or
   only as vocabulary. The expected answer takes two lines.
2. *"Is `2^(n+1) = O(2^n)`? Is `2^(2n) = O(2^n)`?"* — checking whether you apply the definition or
   pattern-match. The first is true with `c = 2, n₀ = 0`, because `2^(n+1) = 2·2^n`. The second is
   false: `2^(2n)/2^n = 2^n` grows without bound, so no constant can cover it.
3. *"Your benchmark fits a straight line up to a million. Is the algorithm linear?"* — checking
   whether you will overclaim from finite evidence. The honest answer names the derivation as the
   primary evidence and the fit as a consistency check.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. State the definition of `f(n) = O(g(n))` with both quantifiers, and name the two numbers.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   *There exist* `c > 0` and `n₀` *such that for all* `n ≥ n₀`, `f(n) ≤ c · g(n)`. The two numbers are
   the multiplier `c` and the threshold `n₀` — together, a witness. Producing them is the whole
   proof.
   </details>

2. Give a witness for `5n³ + 100n² + 9 = O(n³)`.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Take `n₀ = 1`. Then `n² ≤ n³` and `1 ≤ n³`, so `5n³ + 100n² + 9 ≤ 5n³ + 100n³ + 9n³ = 114n³`.
   Witness `(114, 1)`. If a tighter constant is wanted, `(6, 101)` works too — but "tighter" is not
   "more correct", and nothing in the notation records which witness you used.
   </details>

3. Why can a claim not be disproved by finding a single `n` where `f(n) > g(n)`?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because the definition allows a multiplier. One bad point is covered by choosing a larger `c`, and
   any finite set of bad points is covered by choosing a larger `n₀`. To disprove you must show no
   pair works at all — normally by showing `f(n)/g(n)` grows without bound, which is what §6's
   growing "smallest c" column was demonstrating.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** You are given a tool that reports "`f(n) ≤ c·g(n)` verified for all `n` up to ten
   million" for `c = 2`. Construct an `f` and `g` for which that report is true and
   `f(n) = O(g(n))` is false.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Anything whose ratio grows, slowly, from a base below the checked constant. Take
   `f(n) = n · log₂(n) / 10` and `g(n) = n`: the ratio is `log₂(n)/10`, which at `n = 10⁷` is about
   2.3 — so a check to ten million with `c = 2` fails slightly, and with `c = 3` passes comfortably —
   while the ratio grows without bound and no constant works forever. Cruder version:
   `f(n) = n²/10⁷`, `g(n) = n`, whose ratio only reaches 2 at `n = 2 × 10⁷`. The general recipe is to
   take a genuinely faster-growing `f` and divide it by a constant big enough to push the crossover
   past whatever range the tool checks — which is [1.2](../01-from-seconds-to-shapes/1.2-eventual-dominance.md)
   used as a weapon.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-04`.** Yesterday's document said the growth class survives multiplication by a
   constant. Point at the exact part of today's definition that encodes that, and at the part that
   encodes "lower-order terms don't matter".
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The constant `c` encodes invariance under multiplication: if `f = O(g)` then `1000f = O(g)` too,
   with `c` multiplied by a thousand. The threshold `n₀` encodes the lower-order terms: a term that
   grows more slowly is eventually dominated, so there is some point past which it can be absorbed
   into `c · g`. The definition is `CPX-04`'s two discards written as two quantifiers.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** A colleague claims their function is `O(1)` because it does a fixed
   number of Python operations. When is that claim wrong even though the operation count really is
   fixed?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   When an operation's own cost grows with the input — Day 1's leak. `x * y` on integers with `n`
   digits is not constant; `s[:]` on a string of length `n` is not constant; `int(s)` on a numeral of
   length `n` is not constant, and in CPython 3.11+ it raises for very long inputs rather than being
   slow. A fixed number of steps is `O(1)` only under the RAM model's uniform-cost assumption, and
   the claim must be made in a model where that assumption holds.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A pull request adds the comment `# O(1) amortized` above a method, with a benchmark
   showing flat timings up to ten thousand calls. What do you ask?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   What the amortized argument is — a credit invariant or a potential function, not a benchmark —
   and what the *worst single call* costs, because "amortized `O(1)`" permits an occasional `O(n)`
   call, and if this is on a latency-sensitive path the p99 lives in exactly that call. The flat
   benchmark up to ten thousand is consistent with the claim and does not establish it (§8), and it
   would look identical for a structure that resizes every ten thousand and one calls. Day 7's
   accounting and potential methods are where the real argument comes from.
   </details>
