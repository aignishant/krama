# 2.2 — Omega, Theta, and the abuse of O

## 1. The one-line answer

An upper bound says only that the cost is no worse than something, so it can be true and useless at
the same time — the claim that actually describes a procedure is the two-sided one, pinned above and
below.

---

## 2. The story

The management committee of the school in Madurai has been arguing about the new block since
February, and the argument has finally become specific, which is worse.

They need three classrooms and a staff toilet before the June term. Two contractors have submitted
quotations. The correspondent, Selvi Ramanathan, has both of them on the table in front of her and a
bank manager coming on Thursday.

The first quotation is one page and it says: *the work will not exceed eighteen lakh rupees.*

Selvi asks the contractor, twice, what it will cost. He repeats the sentence. It will not exceed
eighteen lakh. He is not being evasive — this is how he has quoted for thirty years, and he is
proud of the number, because it is a promise he can keep: he has never in his life gone over a
ceiling he quoted. What he will not do is tell her it will be twelve, because if the sand rate moves
in April he will have said something untrue.

The trouble is that Selvi cannot do anything with the sentence. She has to go to the bank on
Thursday and ask for a loan. If she asks for eighteen and the work costs eleven, the school pays
interest for four years on seven lakh it never needed, out of a budget that is mostly fee waivers.
If she asks for eleven and the work costs eighteen, there is an open roof over a classroom in the
second week of June, when it rains every afternoon.

The second contractor's quotation is two pages, and the number on it is a range: *between thirteen
and a half and fifteen lakh, depending on the sand rate and whether the old foundation can carry the
second floor.* He walks her through both ends. The low end assumes the foundation holds. The high
end assumes it does not and they underpin.

Selvi takes the second quotation, and the committee's treasurer objects loudly, because the first
number was smaller in the only place he was looking — the top. Selvi's answer is the one worth
keeping: *the first man told me what he will never do. The second man told me what it is going to
cost. I cannot take a loan against what somebody will never do.*

The block is finished on the second of June for fourteen lakh and twenty thousand. The first
contractor kept his promise too, all the way through — he would have been inside eighteen. He was
inside eighteen the whole time. That was never the question.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

A ceiling is a promise. It is not a description, and the difference is exactly the difference
between the two quotations.

`O(g)` is the ceiling: the cost is *at most* a constant times `g`, beyond some point. That is
[2.1](2.1-big-o-and-its-witness.md), and everything in it stands. The thing nobody says out loud
when they teach it is that ceilings are cheap. If a procedure is `O(n)`, it is also `O(n²)`, and
`O(n³)`, and `O(2ⁿ)` — every one of those is a true statement about a linear loop, because each of
them is a promise it keeps easily. "Not more than eighteen lakh" is true of a job that costs
fourteen, and it is also true of a job that costs two.

So the notation needs a floor, and it has one. `Ω(g)` is the lower bound: the cost is *at least* a
constant times `g`, beyond some point. Written the same way, with the inequality turned round.

And when the same `g` works for both — a floor and a ceiling of the same shape — you have pinned the
function between two constant multiples of `g`, and that is `Θ(g)`. It is the two-page quotation:
not less than this, not more than that, and both ends are the same shape. `Θ` is the claim that
actually answers "what does this cost".

Now the abuses, which are the reason this document exists.

**The loose upper bound, offered as an answer.** "What's the complexity of your solution?" — "It's
`O(n²)`." If the loop is linear, that answer is *true*, and it is the answer of somebody who either
does not know or is hedging. In an interview it reads as the second one. Say `Θ` when you can prove
`Θ`, and say `O` when the upper bound is genuinely all you have.

**`O` where `Θ` is meant, by convention.** In practice almost everybody writes "quicksort is
`O(n log n)` on average" when they mean `Θ`. This convention is so widespread that fighting it is
useless; what is not useless is knowing when the distinction is load-bearing. It is load-bearing
whenever the bound is being used to argue that something is *fast enough* — a ceiling supports that
argument — versus that something is *not fast enough*, which needs a floor. You cannot prove a
procedure is too slow with an upper bound.

**"Which case" confused with "which bound".** This is the deep one. *Best, average and worst* are
choices about **which input**. *`O`, `Ω`, `Θ`* are choices about **which direction of bound**. They
are independent, so there are combinations, and every one of them is a different sentence:

|  | `O` (ceiling) | `Ω` (floor) | `Θ` (both) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Best case** | at most this, on the friendliest input | at least this, on the friendliest input | exactly this shape, on the friendliest input |
| **Worst case** | at most this, on every input | at least this, on the nastiest input | exactly this shape, on the nastiest input |

"Linear search is `O(n)`" means: on every input, at most linear. "Linear search is `Ω(1)`" means: on
its best input, it can finish in constant time. Both true. "Linear search is `Θ(n)`" is only true if
you say *in the worst case* — because on the best input it is `Θ(1)`, and §8 measures a gap of more
than a factor of fifty million between those two sentences on one machine.

One more piece of vocabulary, for completeness, because it appears in papers and in nobody's
conversation: `o(g)` — little-o — is the *strict* version of `O`, meaning `f` grows strictly more
slowly, so `f(n)/g(n)` tends to zero. `n = o(n²)` is true; `n² = o(n²)` is false, while
`n² = O(n²)` is true. Its mirror is `ω`. You will use `O`, `Ω` and `Θ` every day and these two about
once a year.

> **Formally:** `f = Ω(g)` iff there exist `c > 0`, `n₀` with `f(n) ≥ c · g(n)` for all `n ≥ n₀`.
> `f = Θ(g)` iff `f = O(g)` and `f = Ω(g)` — equivalently, iff there exist `c₁, c₂ > 0` and `n₀`
> with `c₁ · g(n) ≤ f(n) ≤ c₂ · g(n)` for all `n ≥ n₀`.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **PostgreSQL's `EXPLAIN`, which prints two costs per node, not one.** Run `EXPLAIN SELECT …` and
  every line reads like `cost=0.29..8.31`. The first number is the *startup cost* — work before the
  first row can be emitted — and the second is the *total cost* for all rows. The planner keeps both
  because a single number cannot support the decisions it has to make: for a query wrapped in
  `LIMIT 1`, the startup cost is what matters and a plan with a terrible total cost may be the right
  choice; for an aggregate over everything, only the total matters. A sort node has a large startup
  cost and a small marginal cost per row; an index scan has the reverse. That is a two-sided
  description used operationally, for the reason Selvi needed two numbers.

- **The `Ω(n log n)` lower bound on comparison sorting.** This is a floor, and note what it is a
  floor *on*: not on one algorithm, but on **every possible algorithm** that sorts by comparing
  pairs of elements. The proof is a decision-tree argument — `n!` possible orderings, each comparison
  splits the possibilities in two at best, so at least `log₂(n!) ≈ n log₂ n` comparisons are needed
  — and it is the reason library authors stopped looking for a comparison sort that beats it. It is
  also the reason radix sort is not a counterexample: radix sort does not compare elements, so it is
  outside the model the floor was proved in. Lower bounds are how a field knows when to stop
  optimising, and they always come attached to a model. Day 51 proves this one.

- **Asked as:** *"Is quicksort `O(n²)`?"* The answer is yes — and the follow-up is the actual
  question: yes, in the worst case, on the already-sorted input with a naive pivot; its average is
  `Θ(n log n)` over the randomness of the pivot choice. A candidate who says "no, it's `O(n log n)`"
  has confused the average case with a universal ceiling, and that is the single most common
  complexity error in interviews.

## 5. The mechanism

```
    cost
      ^
      |                                                  c₂·g(n)     <- the ceiling: O
      |                                            .  '
      |                                      .  '   ______
      |                               .  ' ______ --      f(n)
      |                        .  ' _____--      _ _ _ _ _
      |                 .  '  ____--     _ _ _ -            c₁·g(n)  <- the floor: Ω
      |          .  ' ___--    _ _ - -
      |    . '  __--  _ - -
      |  _--  _ -
      +------|------------------------------------------------->  n
             n₀
             |<---- f is trapped between the two, forever ---->
```

*What to notice: `Θ` is not "the average of the two bounds" and it is not "a more precise `O`". It is
the claim that one single shape `g`, scaled by two different constants, traps the function from both
sides. If no such `g` exists — if the function's floor and ceiling have different shapes — then the
function has no `Θ`, and saying only `O` is the honest answer rather than a lazy one.*

**The invariant:** *beyond `n₀`, `c₁·g(n) ≤ f(n) ≤ c₂·g(n)` — for every `n`, in both directions.*

### Trace one: pinning a polynomial

**Claim:** `3n² + 7n + 2 = Θ(n²)`.

*The ceiling*, from [2.1](2.1-big-o-and-its-witness.md): for `n ≥ 1`, `3n² + 7n + 2 ≤ 12n²`, so
`c₂ = 12`.

*The floor*: every term is positive, so for `n ≥ 1`, `3n² + 7n + 2 ≥ 3n²`, so `c₁ = 3`.

Witnesses `(c₁, c₂, n₀) = (3, 12, 1)`, and the claim is proved. Note how much easier the floor was
than the ceiling: for a sum of positive terms, throwing terms *away* gives a lower bound
immediately. That asymmetry is why people who can do `O` in their sleep freeze on `Ω` — it is
usually the easier half, and looks harder only because it is practised less.

### Trace two: the same procedure, three different answers

`target in xs`, a linear scan of a list of a million integers, measured on this machine:

| Case | Input | Measured | Bound |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Best | `target` at index 0 | 0.000 µs — below the timer's resolution | `Θ(1)` |
| Middle | `target` at index 500 000 | 2 777.5 µs | `Θ(n)` |
| Worst | `target` absent | 5 583.2 µs | `Θ(n)` |

One procedure, three measurements, and the correct single-sentence summary is: *`O(n)` over all
inputs, `Ω(1)` over all inputs, `Θ(n)` in the worst case, `Θ(1)` in the best case.* Every one of
those clauses is a different claim, and dropping the case makes the sentence unfalsifiable — which
is why "what is the complexity of linear search" is not, strictly, a well-formed question.

## 6. Line by line

The instrument for a two-sided claim has to look at both sides, so it computes both extremes of the
ratio `f(n)/g(n)` over the range you have.

```python
from collections.abc import Callable

def sandwich(f: Callable[[int], float], g: Callable[[int], float],
             n0: int, limit: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Return (c1, c2): the tightest floor and ceiling multipliers over [n0, limit]."""
    ratios = [f(n) / g(n) for n in range(n0, limit + 1)]
    return min(ratios), max(ratios)
```

Both ends, from one set of ratios. If `c₁` and `c₂` come out close to each other and stay close as
you extend the range, `Θ(g)` is plausible; if the gap between them widens with the range, `g` is the
wrong shape and one of the two bounds is loose. The *ratio between the two constants* is the signal,
not either constant on its own.

```python
def sandwich_streaming(f, g, n0: int, limit: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    low = high = f(n0) / g(n0)
    for n in range(n0 + 1, limit + 1):
        ratio = f(n) / g(n)
        low = min(low, ratio)
        high = max(high, ratio)
    return low, high
```

The same answer without the list. This is not a micro-optimisation: the first version is `Θ(limit)`
in space for a computation that needs `Θ(1)`, and at a limit of ten million that is the difference
between a fold and a memory error. The habit of asking "does this need to hold all the values, or
just the running summary?" is worth having early — Phase 28's streaming algorithms are that question
asked seriously.

```python
    return low, high
```

Two numbers returned, deliberately, where one would have been more convenient. A function that
returned only `high` would be usable and would quietly train its callers to think in ceilings, which
is the habit this entire document is written against. Make the API return both and the caller has to
look at both.

**The near-miss.** Here is the version that summarises instead of bounding:

```python
def average_ratio(f, g, n0: int, limit: int) -> float:
    ratios = [f(n) / g(n) for n in range(n0, limit + 1)]
    return sum(ratios) / len(ratios)          # "the constant, on average"
```

It looks more robust — one number, less sensitive to a single outlier — and it destroys the only
thing the check was for. Take `f(n) = n·log₂n` and `g(n) = n` over `n` in `[2, 1024]`: the ratios run
from 1 to 10, the average comes out around 8.6, and the function reports a tidy constant for a pair
that has no constant at all. The input that kills it is any `f` whose ratio grows *slowly*: an
average smears the growth into a number that looks like a bound, where `min` and `max` would have
shown you 1 and 10 and made the growth obvious. When you are testing whether something is bounded,
never average — averaging is the operation that makes unbounded things look bounded.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation.**

`sandwich_streaming` evaluates `f` and `g`, divides, and does two comparisons per value of `n`.
With `m = limit - n₀ + 1` values and `c_step` the fixed per-value work:

```
T(m) = Σ (i = 1 … m) c_step  =  c_step · m  =  Θ(m)
```

The list version does the same work plus one append per value and one full pass each for `min` and
`max`, so `3m` passes' worth of memory traffic against one — same class, three times the constant,
and `Θ(m)` space instead of `Θ(1)`. Both are `Θ(m)` in time and only one of them can be run on a
range of ten million, which is [1.3](../01-from-seconds-to-shapes/1.3-what-dropping-constants-destroys.md)'s
point in one line.

Now the derivation that actually matters today — the three cases of the linear scan in §5. Let `n`
be the list's length and `c` the per-element cost:

```
best     T(n) = c · 1                          target at index 0
worst    T(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) c  =  c · n      target absent
average  E[T(n)] = Σ (j = 1 … n) j · (1/n) · c  =  c · (n + 1) / 2
```

The average uses **expectation** over an explicitly stated probability space — the target is present
and uniformly distributed over the `n` positions — exactly as Day 2's 1.2 derived it. Change the
distribution and the number changes: if nine in ten lookups are misses, the expected cost is close to
`c · n` and the "half the list" figure is a fiction.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it | Bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best | `Θ(1)` | Target at index 0 | also `Ω(1)` for the algorithm |
| Average | `Θ(n)` | Target present, position uniform | — |
| Worst | `Θ(n)` | Target absent | also `O(n)` for the algorithm |

Read the right-hand column carefully, because it is the point of the document: the algorithm's
universal ceiling `O(n)` comes from the worst case, and its universal floor `Ω(1)` comes from the
best case. There is no `Θ` for "the algorithm" at all — only per case.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary for the scan.

**What the constant hides.** The measured best case in §5 is 0.000 µs, which is not zero — it is
below `perf_counter`'s resolution of roughly 100 ns. A "constant-time" measurement that reports zero
is reporting the instrument's floor, and the correct way to measure it is to run the operation a
million times and divide. Any benchmark whose best case reads 0.000 is measuring the clock.

## 8. When it breaks

Here is the same function measured three ways, on one machine, in one session:

```
`target in xs` on a list of 1,000,000 ints, best of 200 runs
  target at index 0        :        0.000 us
  target at index 500,000  :     2777.500 us
  target absent            :     5583.200 us
```

The gap between the first row and the third is a factor of at least fifty million, and both rows are
honest measurements of the same operation. Now imagine the benchmark that produced only the first
row — a perfectly ordinary mistake, because the natural test data is a small list and the natural
target is something near the front — and the design document that says, on that evidence, *"list
membership: about a microsecond, negligible"*. Nothing in that sentence is a lie about the
measurement. Everything about it is wrong as a prediction.

The doubling table settles it, and it is what should have been run instead:

```
the same search, doubling n, target absent
  n =    250000       1.378 ms   ratio      -
  n =    500000       2.784 ms   ratio   2.02
  n =   1000000       5.574 ms   ratio   2.00
  n =   2000000      12.935 ms   ratio   2.32
  n =   4000000      25.600 ms   ratio   1.98
```

Ratios of 2 — linear in the worst case, and no amount of measuring the best case would have shown
it.

The second failure is verbal rather than numerical, and it is the one that costs marks. Someone
asserts "this is `O(n log n)`" about a routine that is quadratic in the worst case, having measured
only random inputs. The `O` claim is then simply **false** — not loose, false — because `O` is a
claim about *every* input beyond `n₀`, and a single family of inputs that grows faster refutes it.
The confusion is treating `O` as though it meant "typically about". It does not: `O` is universal
over inputs and `Θ`-on-the-average-case is the sentence that means "typically about".

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is to benchmark the worst case too. The fix is
a sentence template you use for the rest of the curriculum, and it has three slots:

> **"Θ(what), in which case, over which inputs."**

*"`Θ(n)` in the worst case, over any list, where `n` is the list's length."* Fill all three slots and
almost every error in this document becomes impossible to state. Leave the case out and you have
said something unfalsifiable; leave the "over which inputs" out and you have not said what `n` is,
which is Day 1's `CPX-02`. The reason this is a fix and not a patch is that it changes what you
*notice*: once the template is a habit, an unqualified "it's O(n)" in a code review reads as an
incomplete sentence rather than as an answer.

## 9. In production

**At scale, the worst case stops being hypothetical.** This is the practical reason to care about
the distinction between a ceiling and a description.

*At a billion requests, a one-in-a-million input happens a thousand times a day.* Tail latency is
literally the worst case, sampled. A service whose average is `Θ(1)` and whose worst case is `Θ(n)`
is a service with a p99.9 that tracks your largest customer's data, and those are exactly the users
whose experience you cannot afford to lose. "It's fast on average" is a statement about the mean of
a distribution, and users do not experience the mean.

*Adversaries choose your inputs.* The worst case is not a rare accident when somebody is trying to
cause it. Algorithmic complexity attacks — feeding a service the inputs that hit its quadratic path
— are a real denial-of-service class, and the defence is architectural: bound the worst case, or
randomise so that the attacker cannot construct it. It is why hash functions in language runtimes are
seeded randomly at process start, and why sorting libraries use introsort, which detects a bad
recursion depth and switches algorithms. Both are engineering responses to the gap between the
average case and the worst case.

*Lower bounds tell you when to stop.* The most expensive thing an engineering team can do is optimise
towards an impossibility. Knowing that comparison sorting is `Ω(n log n)`, that any algorithm must
read its input at least once for problems whose answer depends on all of it, or that a problem is
NP-hard, converts "we need a smarter algorithm" into "we need a different problem" — a weaker
guarantee, an approximation, a precomputation, a cache. That conversation is a floor being used
correctly, and Phase 29 is eight days of it.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "This says `O(n)` and I believe you, but `O` isn't what I need here. What's the *worst* case, on
> our data, and what's the input that produces it? If the honest answer is that this is `Θ(1)` on
> everything except a customer with fifty thousand items in one cart, then say that in the comment,
> because that customer exists and support will find them before we do."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you state a complexity:

1. *"Is that a tight bound?"* — checking whether you know `O` from `Θ`, and whether you will claim
   tightness you cannot prove. The right answer is either a `Θ` with a floor argument, or "no, `O` is
   all I can show, and here is why the floor is harder".
2. *"Which case is that?"* — checking the case-versus-bound distinction. If you gave one number for
   an algorithm with an early exit, this question is a hint that you left a slot empty.
3. *"Can any algorithm do better?"* — checking whether you can think in lower bounds at all. For a
   problem that must inspect its whole input the answer is `Ω(n)` and the argument is two sentences;
   for comparison sorting it is `Ω(n log n)` and the argument is the decision tree.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Is a linear loop `O(n²)`? Is it `Θ(n²)`? Is it `Ω(1)`?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Yes, no, yes. `O(n²)` is a true ceiling — witness `c = 1, n₀ = 1` since `n ≤ n²` — and it is
   useless. `Θ(n²)` is false, because the floor fails: `n ≥ c·n²` cannot hold for large `n` with any
   positive `c`. `Ω(1)` is true and equally useless. The interesting statement is the tight one,
   `Θ(n)`.
   </details>

2. Give the two-sided witnesses for `n² + 100n = Θ(n²)`.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Ceiling: for `n ≥ 1`, `100n ≤ 100n²`, so `n² + 100n ≤ 101n²`, giving `c₂ = 101`. Floor: all terms
   positive, so `n² + 100n ≥ n²`, giving `c₁ = 1`. Witnesses `(1, 101, 1)`. If you want a tighter
   ceiling, `c₂ = 2` works from `n ≥ 100`.
   </details>

3. Why is "quicksort is `O(n log n)`" false while "quicksort is `Θ(n log n)` on average" is true?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because `O` quantifies over *every* input beyond `n₀`, and quicksort with a fixed pivot rule has
   inputs — the already-sorted list, for the naive first-element pivot — on which it performs
   `Θ(n²)` comparisons. One family of inputs growing faster than `n log n` refutes the ceiling
   outright. The average-case claim is a different claim entirely: it is about the expectation over a
   stated distribution, which is a fourth thing alongside best, worst, and the bound direction.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** A colleague's tool reports `c₁ = 0.98` and `c₂ = 1.02` for `f` against `g` over
   `n ∈ [1, 10⁶]` and concludes `f = Θ(g)`. Construct an `f` and `g` for which that report is
   produced and the conclusion is false.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Make the divergence start past the checked range: `f(n) = n` for `n ≤ 10⁶` and `f(n) = n²` beyond,
   with `g(n) = n`. That is contrived but it is exactly what a real cost function does when a data
   structure outgrows a cache, a table spills to disk, or an integer stops fitting in a machine word
   — the shape genuinely changes at a threshold, and the threshold is often just past the range
   anybody tested. A less contrived version: `f(n) = n · (1 + log₂(n)/10⁶)`, whose ratio to `n` is
   within 2% of 1 across the whole checked range and grows without bound. This is
   [2.1](2.1-big-o-and-its-witness.md) §8 again: a finite check refutes, and never establishes.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-04`.** Yesterday's `1.2` said a faster-growing function eventually dominates.
   Restate that in today's notation, in both directions.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   "`g` eventually dominates `f`" is `f = O(g)` — with the constant folded in, since the crossover
   may need one. The strict version, where `g` grows genuinely faster rather than merely no more
   slowly, is `f = o(g)`, equivalently `f(n)/g(n) → 0`. Turned around, `g = Ω(f)`, and if neither
   dominates strictly, `f = Θ(g)`. The crossover point from that document is, in this document's
   vocabulary, the `n₀` in the witness — which is why the definition needed one at all.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-03`.** Day 2 said a variant bounds the iteration count. Which of `O`, `Ω`, `Θ`
   does a termination proof give you, and why can it not give you the others?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   `O` only. A variant that starts at `V₀` and falls by at least one per iteration proves the loop
   runs *at most* `V₀` times — a ceiling. It says nothing about a floor, because the loop may exit far
   earlier: Day 2's halving loop had measure `n` and ran `log₂ n` times, so the `O(n)` from the
   variant was true and enormously loose. To get `Ω` you need an argument that the loop cannot exit
   early — usually the negated guard plus something about the data — and only then can the two be
   combined into `Θ`.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A design document says "the sync job is `O(n)` in the number of records, so it will
   scale". What do you write in the margin?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Three things. *Which case, and over which inputs* — `O(n)` on every input, or on the ones we have
   seen? *What is `n`, and what is the constant* — `Θ(n)` at 40 ms a record is eleven hours for a
   million records, and "scales" is not the word for that; this is
   [1.3](../01-from-seconds-to-shapes/1.3-what-dropping-constants-destroys.md). And *what is the
   floor* — if the job must read every record, it is also `Ω(n)`, which means no amount of
   optimisation inside the loop changes the shape and the only real lever is doing it incrementally
   or in parallel. That last point is the one that changes the design, and it comes from the bound
   people never state.
   </details>
