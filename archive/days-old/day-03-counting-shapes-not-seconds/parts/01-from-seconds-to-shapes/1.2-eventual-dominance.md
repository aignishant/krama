# 1.2 — Eventual dominance and the crossover

## 1. The one-line answer

Saying one procedure grows faster than another is a claim about what happens once the input is big
enough, and the size at which it starts being true — the crossover — can be far past anything you
will ever run.

---

## 2. The story

Ratan Hardware has been on the corner of that road in Bhopal since Girish Patil's father opened it,
and it has always bought its cement on credit, because everybody buys cement on credit.

There are two suppliers now, and both of them want the account.

The old firm charges the way the old firm has always charged: three thousand rupees added to the
outstanding at the end of every month, no matter how much is outstanding. A flat plate of ghee on
the bill, Girish's father calls it.

The new firm has a smarter offer and a young man in a pressed shirt to explain it. No flat charge
at all — just four percent a month on whatever is still owed. Four percent. He says it twice,
lightly, the way you say a small number.

Girish does the arithmetic on the back of a delivery challan for the account he actually runs, a
standing outstanding of about fifty thousand rupees. Month one: the old firm wants three thousand,
the new firm wants two. Month two, if he settles nothing: the old firm has taken six thousand, the
new firm four thousand and eighty. After a year the old firm has taken thirty-six thousand and the
new firm thirty. The young man is winning, and knows it, and the sum is not close.

His father, who is seventy-nine and sits by the door, asks one question. Not "how much this month".
He asks: *and if the account is never settled for five years?*

Girish runs it again, and this time it takes him a while, because the second column does something
the first one does not: it grows on what it has already grown. After five years the flat charge has
added a hundred and eighty thousand rupees — the same three thousand, sixty times over. The four
percent has added four hundred and seventy-six thousand. On a fifty thousand rupee outstanding.

Somewhere between the twelfth month and the sixtieth, the two charges crossed. Girish finds the
month: it is the twenty-first. Before it the smart offer is genuinely cheaper, and if the account is
always cleared inside a year and a half, the smart offer is the right offer and the old firm's flat
plate is just a plate of ghee. After it, nothing about the account can be described as an offer at
all.

His father says the thing he has said in that shop for fifty years, and it sounds like superstition
until you have seen the two columns side by side: *the small percentage is the expensive one. Wait
and see.*

Both men are right, and that is the part worth taking away. What decides which of them is right
today is not the interest rates. It is how long the account stays open.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

Two cost functions, and which one is smaller depends entirely on where you look.

Say that `g` **eventually dominates** `f` when there is some size `n₀` such that for every input size
at least that large, `g(n)` is at least `f(n)`. The word doing the work is *eventually*: the claim
says nothing at all about small inputs, and it does not have to. The compounding charge dominates
the flat charge, and the flat charge is the more expensive one for twenty months.

The size at which the ordering flips has a name too — the **crossover point** — and it is the number
that actually decides what you ship. "Asymptotically better" tells you which side of the crossover
you would rather be on if the input keeps growing. It does not tell you which side you are on.

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is why this is a document
rather than a sentence.

The first is that asymptotics can be ignored, legitimately, when your inputs are small and stay
small. A procedure that is `Θ(n²)` with a tiny constant is the right choice for `n = 30` if the
alternative is `Θ(n log n)` with a fat one, and §5 measures a real pair where exactly that is true
up to about a hundred elements. The standard libraries you use every day do this deliberately.

The second is that "our inputs are small" is a claim with an expiry date. Data grows; the flat
charge does not become expensive, and the four percent does not become cheap. Every system that
fell over on a Tuesday because a table crossed some size was running code chosen on the correct
side of a crossover that later moved. The right posture is not to pick a side; it is to know where
the crossover is, and to know which side of it you are on today and next year.

> **Formally:** `g` eventually dominates `f` when there exists `n₀ ∈ ℕ` such that `f(n) ≤ g(n)` for
> all `n ≥ n₀`. The smallest such `n₀` is the crossover point. Equivalently, when the limit of
> `f(n)/g(n)` as `n → ∞` is zero, `g` eventually dominates `f` — and the rate at which that limit
> approaches zero says nothing about how large `n₀` is.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **CPython's `list.sort`, and the constant `MINRUN`.** Timsort does not run its merging machinery on
  short stretches. It finds natural runs in the data and, when a run is shorter than `MINRUN` —
  computed to be between 32 and 64 — it extends it to that length using **binary insertion sort**,
  which is `Θ(k²)` in the number of element moves. An asymptotically inferior algorithm, chosen on
  purpose, because below a few dozen elements its constant beats merge sort's. The same decision is
  in the C++ standard library: libstdc++'s introsort has `_S_threshold = 16` and finishes with
  insertion sort. If you take one production fact from this document, take this one: the sorting
  routines in the languages you use are hybrids, and the thresholds in them are measured crossovers.

- **Strassen's matrix multiplication, and why BLAS does not start with it.** Strassen's algorithm
  multiplies `n × n` matrices in about `n^2.807` operations against the schoolbook `n³`, and has done
  since 1969. Production linear-algebra libraries use it, if at all, only above a size threshold in
  the high hundreds or thousands, because its constant is larger, its memory traffic is worse, and
  its recursive structure is unfriendly to the cache blocking that makes the `n³` version run near
  peak hardware throughput. An algorithm can be asymptotically better for fifty years and still be
  the wrong call at your size.

- **Asked as:** *"Which of these two solutions is better?"* — with the input constraint written at
  the top of the problem, where the candidate did not look. When the header says `n ≤ 100`, the
  `Θ(n³)` solution is not merely acceptable, it is often the intended one, and reaching for a clever
  `Θ(n log n)` is the wrong engineering answer with the right vocabulary attached. Day 4's 3.1 is
  about reading that constraint deliberately.

## 5. The mechanism

Take two real procedures, both written in Python, measured on this machine.

*Fat linear:* a single pass over `n` elements doing genuine per-element work — formatting each value
into a string and parsing two integers back out of it. Its cost is `c₁·n` with a very large `c₁`.

*Thin quadratic:* `n` calls to `list.count`, each of which scans the whole list at C speed. Its cost
is `c₂·n²` with a very small `c₂`.

```
        microseconds
             |
      18000  |                                                        Q
             |                                                    .
             |                                              .
       4500  |                                        Q
             |                              .
             |                     .
       1100  |             Q                                     L
        600  |        .                              L
        300  |    Q                        L
        150  |  Q  L
         70  | QL          <- crossover: about n = 108
         20  |QL
              +----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----+----
              10   50  100  150  200  300  400  800 1600      n

              Q = thin quadratic     L = fat linear
```

*What to notice: to the left of the crossover the quadratic is not slightly better, it is nearly
eight times better at `n = 10`. To the right it is not slightly worse; by `n = 1600` it is fifteen
times worse and the gap is still opening. Both facts are about the same pair of procedures.*

**The invariant:** *once the faster-growing function has overtaken, it stays overtaken.* Past the
crossover the ratio between the two costs grows without bound and never returns, which is what makes
"eventually dominates" a useful thing to know and not merely a true thing to say. It is also why the
crossover is a *point* rather than a region: for these shapes the ordering flips exactly once.

### The measured table

| `n` | fat linear (µs) | thin quadratic (µs) | winner |
|---:|---:|---:|:--|
| 10 | 7.82 | 1.00 | quadratic |
| 25 | 19.30 | 4.99 | quadratic |
| 50 | 38.02 | 18.11 | quadratic |
| 100 | 77.34 | 69.88 | quadratic |
| 150 | 117.50 | 158.70 | linear |
| 200 | 157.18 | 269.23 | linear |
| 400 | 313.45 | 1 110.68 | linear |
| 800 | 628.45 | 4 516.66 | linear |
| 1600 | 1 231.59 | 18 254.67 | linear |

The flip happens between 100 and 150. §7 predicts 108 from the constants alone, before running
anything in that range — which is the point of being able to derive rather than only measure.

## 6. Line by line

What you do with a crossover, in practice, is build a hybrid: use the small-input procedure below a
threshold and the large-input one above it. This is Timsort's `MINRUN` in miniature.

```python
THRESHOLD = 108   # measured on this machine; see calibrate() below

def solve(xs: list[int]) -> int:
    if len(xs) < THRESHOLD:
        return thin_quadratic(xs)
    return fat_linear(xs)
```

Three lines, and the only interesting thing about them is the constant. Note that it is a named
module-level constant and not a literal buried in the `if` — because it is *data*, obtained by
measurement, that will be wrong on another machine, and a reader has to be able to find it and ask
where it came from.

```python
def calibrate(low: int = 8, high: int = 4096) -> int:
    """The smallest size at or above which fat_linear wins, found by doubling."""
    n = low
    while n <= high:
        if best_of(fat_linear, build(n)) < best_of(thin_quadratic, build(n)):
            return n
        n *= 2
    return high
```

The search doubles rather than stepping by one, for the reason `CPX-02` gives: stepping to a
threshold near four thousand means four thousand measurements, and each measurement at size `n`
costs `Θ(n²)` for the quadratic side. Doubling makes it twelve measurements, and the total cost is
dominated by the last one — the geometric-sum argument from
[1.1](1.1-seconds-do-not-travel.md) §7, used for a second time in a different setting.

```python
    return low if best_of(fat_linear, build(low)) < best_of(thin_quadratic, build(low)) else n
```

The boundary case matters and is easy to get wrong: if the linear version already wins at the
smallest size tested, the crossover is at or below `low`, and returning `low` is honest. Returning
`high` — the "I did not find it" answer — would silently install a threshold of four thousand and
run the wrong procedure for every input in between. When a calibration fails, it must fail in the
direction that is merely slow, never in the direction that is quietly wrong.

**The near-miss.** Here is the calibration everyone writes first, and it is wrong in a way that
takes months to show up:

```python
THRESHOLD = 108     # measured once, on a laptop, on random data, in 2026
```

Three separate problems, each fatal on its own.

*It was measured on one machine.* The crossover is the ratio of two constants, and both constants
are machine-dependent. A build server with a different cache size, a different CPU, or a debug build
of the interpreter has a different crossover — often by a factor of two or three.

*It was measured on one kind of data.* The quadratic procedure here is `list.count`, whose real cost
depends on nothing but length. Substitute any procedure with a data-dependent cost — a sort, a
search, anything with an early exit — and the crossover for random data is not the crossover for
sorted data. The input that kills this threshold is not large; it is *differently shaped*.

*It was measured once.* Both implementations will be edited. A change that makes `fat_linear` twice
as fast moves the crossover by a factor of two, and nothing in the code will tell you, because the
threshold is a number that no test asserts on. Thresholds obtained by measurement need a test that
re-derives them, or at minimum a comment naming the machine, the data, and the date — and CPython's
`listobject.c` has exactly that comment above `MINRUN`.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation**, then a single equation.

Fat linear performs a fixed amount of work per element, so its cost is a sum of `n` equal terms:

```
T_L(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) c₁  =  c₁ · n
```

Thin quadratic performs a full scan for each of `n` values, so it is a sum of `n` sums:

```
T_Q(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) ( Σ (j = 1 … n) c₂ )  =  c₂ · n²
```

The crossover is where they are equal:

```
c₁ · n  =  c₂ · n²     ⟹     n* = c₁ / c₂
```

Now put the measured constants in. From the largest rows, where the measurement is most reliable:

```
c₁ = 1231.59 µs / 1600 elements      = 0.7697 µs per element
c₂ = 18254.67 µs / 1600² pairs       = 0.007131 µs per pair

n* = 0.7697 / 0.007131 = 108
```

The measured table flips between 100 and 150. The prediction — made from two rows at `n = 1600`,
with no measurement anywhere near the crossover — lands inside that gap. That is what "derive, then
check" buys you: the crossover of any two shapes is the ratio of their constants, and you can find
it without hunting for it.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `Θ(n)`, the linear branch | Any `n ≥ 108` |
| Average | whichever branch the size selects | — |
| Worst | `Θ(n²)`, the quadratic branch, bounded by `c₂ · 108²` | Any `n < 108` |

The last row is the one people miss: the hybrid's quadratic branch is *bounded*, because it only
ever runs on inputs below the threshold. A quadratic that can never see more than 108 elements costs
at most about 83 microseconds and is therefore, for cost purposes, a constant. That is the real
reason hybrids are safe, and it is why Timsort can call an `O(k²)` sort without apology.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary for both branches here. The hybrid adds nothing but the comparison.

**What the constant hides.** Everything, in this document — the constants *are* the subject. But
note the specific thing hidden inside `c₂`: `list.count` is a C loop, so `c₂` is roughly the cost of
one machine-level comparison, while `c₁` includes interpreter dispatch, string formatting and two
integer parses. The ratio of a hundred and eight between them is not a fact about algorithms at all.
It is a fact about which parts of your program run in C, and it would be a different number in a
compiled language — where, with both procedures compiled, the crossover would fall to somewhere
around ten and the quadratic version would stop being interesting.

## 8. When it breaks

The failure is choosing by asymptotics alone, and it produces no error message — only a program that
is eight times slower than the one you rejected. Here are the two smallest rows of the measured
table again, with the "better" algorithm losing:

```
       n   fat linear (us)   thin quadratic (us)     winner
      10              7.82                  1.00  quadratic
      25             19.30                  4.99  quadratic
      50             38.02                 18.11  quadratic
```

`Θ(n)` beaten by `Θ(n²)`, by 7.8× at `n = 10` and by 2.1× at `n = 50`. Nothing here is a bug.
Both procedures are correct, both derivations are right, and the asymptotic claim — that the
quadratic one eventually dominates — is true and will assert itself at 108. The mistake was
answering a question about `n = 10` with a fact about large `n`.

The reverse mistake costs more and is more common. Ship the quadratic because "our inputs are always
tiny", let two years pass, and read the last row instead:

```
    1600           1231.59              18254.67     linear
```

Fifteen times slower and widening. The input never changed shape; it just got bigger, exactly as
Girish's account got older. This is the failure that arrives as an incident report rather than as a
benchmark.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is to pick the other procedure. The fix is to
make the choice *explicit and inspectable*: measure the crossover, write the hybrid, name the
threshold, and record next to it the machine and the data it was measured on. Then the question "are
we on the right side of the crossover?" has an answer that a reader can check, rather than being a
decision somebody made once and did not write down. And keep the second procedure in the codebase:
the branch you never take is what makes the growth of your input survivable, and deleting it is how
a system ends up with only the fast-at-small-n option on the day the data doubles.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** The crossover is not a constant of nature, and three separate forces move it.

*Hardware moves it.* Cache sizes, memory bandwidth and SIMD width change the constants and therefore
the ratio between them. The famous case is that as caches grew, algorithms with good locality got
relatively cheaper, and several textbook-optimal structures lost their real-world advantage —
Fibonacci heaps have a better bound than binary heaps for decrease-key and lose to them almost
everywhere in practice, because the bound was in operation counts and the loss is in pointer chasing
and cache misses. When you meet that on Day 78, remember it started here.

*Distribution moves it, and averages hide it.* If ninety-nine percent of your inputs are below the
crossover and one percent are far above it, the mean latency is fine and the p99 is a fire. Choosing
the small-input procedure is then a choice to be slow *precisely for your largest customers*, which
is the population most likely to be paying you. Every crossover decision should be made against the
input-size distribution, not the input-size average, and the honest artefact is a histogram.

*Growth moves it, in one direction.* Nobody's data gets smaller. A threshold chosen when the table
had ten thousand rows is a bet that the table stays around ten thousand rows, and that bet is one of
the most reliably lost bets in this industry. When the choice is between a procedure that is
comfortable now and one that stays comfortable, and the difference at today's size is small, take
the second — you are buying an option on your own growth, and it is cheap here and expensive later.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "Where did 108 come from? If it's a measured crossover, say so above the constant — machine, data,
> date — the way CPython documents `MINRUN`. Right now it reads like a magic number and the next
> person will 'clean it up' to 100 or to `len(xs) // 2` and never know they moved it. And this was
> measured on random input; our production data arrives nearly sorted, so I'd want the calibration
> rerun on a real sample before we trust the branch."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you correctly identify the asymptotically better algorithm:

1. *"The constraint says `n ≤ 50`. Does your answer change?"* — checking whether you can abandon the
   clever solution when the size says to. This is the most common failure among strong candidates:
   they optimise past the constraint and spend their time debugging an algorithm the problem did not
   need.
2. *"Why does CPython's sort use insertion sort on short runs?"* — checking whether you know the
   crossover exists in real code, and whether you can say what "asymptotically worse" costs you when
   the input can never exceed 64 elements. It costs a bounded constant.
3. *"Your hybrid's threshold is 108. What would make that number wrong tomorrow?"* — checking
   whether you understand the threshold as a measurement rather than a property: a different machine,
   differently shaped data, or an edit to either branch.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Restate "`g` eventually dominates `f`" in the form of the quantifiers, and say which quantifier
   people forget.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   *There exists* an `n₀` *such that for all* `n ≥ n₀`, `f(n) ≤ g(n)`. The forgotten one is the
   existential: `n₀` can be any size at all, including one far beyond any input you will ever run,
   and the definition places no limit on it. That is the whole content of Girish's twenty months.
   </details>

2. A colleague says their `Θ(n log n)` solution is better than your `Θ(n²)` one. What is the one
   question you ask before agreeing?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   "How big is `n`?" — or, more precisely, "what is the distribution of `n`, now and in two years?"
   Their claim is true about the tail. Whether it is true about the inputs your program actually
   receives is a separate question with a numeric answer, and the crossover is where the two answers
   meet.
   </details>

3. §7 predicted the crossover at 108 without measuring anything near 108. How?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   By deriving both cost functions to their constants — `c₁·n` and `c₂·n²` — measuring `c₁` and `c₂`
   from a single large row each, and solving `c₁n = c₂n²` for `n = c₁/c₂`. The crossover of any two
   shapes is a ratio of constants; you locate it by pricing the shapes, not by hunting along the
   input axis.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** The hybrid in §6 uses the quadratic branch below 108 and the linear branch above.
   Construct a workload on which this hybrid is *slower* than always using the linear branch, without
   changing either procedure.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Call it many times with sizes just under the threshold — say a million calls at `n = 107`. Each
   call takes the quadratic branch, which at 107 elements is right at the point where the two are
   nearly equal, so the hybrid saves nothing and pays for the branch and the calibration's staleness.
   Worse, move to a machine where the real crossover is 40: now every call between 40 and 107 takes
   the *wrong* branch, and the hybrid is systematically slower than always-linear across that whole
   band. The hybrid's benefit is bounded by the gap between the two curves below the threshold, which
   is largest at tiny `n` and vanishes as you approach the crossover — so a workload concentrated
   just below the threshold gets all of the risk and none of the benefit.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** Both procedures in §5 do work proportional to a simple count of steps,
   yet their constants differ by a factor of a hundred. Explain that factor in terms of Day 1's rule
   about what may be charged as one operation.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The RAM model charges every operation the same, and these two procedures' "operations" are not
   the same size. The quadratic one's inner step is a single C-level comparison inside `list.count`;
   the linear one's step is interpreter dispatch plus a string format plus two integer parses. Both
   are one step in the model, and one of them costs a hundred times more on the real machine. The
   model got the *shapes* right — `n` and `n²` — and said nothing about the constants, which is
   exactly the division of labour Day 1's 2.3 warned about and this document depends on.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-03`.** You are asked to calibrate a threshold between two procedures where one
   of them has an early exit. Why is `calibrate` in §6 not sufficient, and what would you change?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because a procedure with a data-dependent exit has no single cost at a given size — it has a best,
   an average and a worst, and which one you measure depends on the data `build(n)` produces. Day 2's
   1.2 derived exactly this for linear search: the target's position determines the iteration count.
   Calibrating on random data installs a threshold that is wrong for sorted data, wrong for
   all-equal data, and wrong for the adversarial case. The change is to calibrate per input *shape*
   and either take the worst-case crossover or dispatch on shape as well as size.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A pull request replaces a `Θ(n²)` routine with a `Θ(n log n)` one, adds 200 lines,
   and the benchmark in the description shows a 3% improvement. What do you say?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   That a 3% improvement at the measured size means the benchmark was run below or near the
   crossover, so the change bought almost nothing today and cost 200 lines of complexity to maintain.
   Then the second question, which decides it: what does the input-size distribution look like in a
   year? If sizes are growing, the change is right and the benchmark is simply measuring the wrong
   size — ask for a table at 10×, 100×, 1000× where the gap should be enormous. If sizes are capped
   by something real — a page of results, a batch limit, a form field — the change is complexity
   bought against growth that cannot happen, and the honest answer is no.
   </details>
