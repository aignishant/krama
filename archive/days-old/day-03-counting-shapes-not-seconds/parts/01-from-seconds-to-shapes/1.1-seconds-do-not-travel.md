# 1.1 — Seconds do not travel

## 1. The one-line answer

A measurement tells you what one machine did on one afternoon with one input, and the thing that
survives a change of machine is not the number of seconds but the way the work grows when the input
gets bigger.

---

## 2. The story

The demonstration room at the municipal corporation in Nagpur has one window, eleven plastic
chairs, and a projector that has to be hit to start.

Arun Deshmukh is buying a property-tax billing system, and two vendors are here to show him one.
The first has come with a laptop that costs more than Arun's motorcycle. They load five hundred
sample properties, press the button, and the bills appear before the room has finished watching:
one and a half seconds. There is a small round of applause from their own side of the table.

The second vendor has brought a desktop tower with a fan that sounds like a scooter. Same five
hundred properties, same button. Eleven seconds. Somebody in the back row laughs.

Two years ago Arun would have signed with the first vendor that afternoon. He did, in fact, sign
with a first vendor that afternoon, on a different tender, on the strength of a demonstration
exactly like this one, and in the third week of the billing cycle — when every ward had been loaded
and the operators were working two shifts — the bills stopped coming out. Not slowly. They stopped.
The corporation printed that cycle's demands by hand and the newspapers had a fine time with it.

So today Arun does something the vendors did not expect, and neither of them likes.

He asks them to run it again with a thousand properties. Then two thousand. Then four. Then eight.
He does not write down the seconds. In the notebook, next to each run, he writes down how much
bigger the time got when the load doubled.

The expensive laptop: one and a half seconds, then six, then twenty-four, then a hundred and one.
Four times the work for twice the properties, every single time.

The scooter-fan desktop: eleven, then twenty-two, then forty-five, then eighty-nine. Twice the
work for twice the properties, every single time.

The corporation has four hundred thousand properties.

Arun does not need either vendor's machine to finish the arithmetic; he only needs the two columns
he has just written down. Doubling from five hundred to four hundred thousand is roughly ten
doublings. The scooter machine's time gets multiplied by two, ten times over. The expensive
laptop's gets multiplied by four, ten times over — and one of those numbers has six more digits in
it than the other.

He thanks the first vendor for their time. The room thinks he is being sentimental about buying
Indian hardware. He is not being sentimental about anything: he has stopped comparing the machines
and started comparing the programs, and the only way to tell those apart in a room like this is to
change the size of the work and watch what happens.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

Two vendors, two machines, two sets of seconds — and none of those seconds mean anything on their
own, because a measured time is a fact about a *run*, not about a *program*. It is a fact about the
processor, the memory, the interpreter version, what else was running, how warm the cache was, how
warm the room was, and which particular input was used. Change any of those and the number changes.
Change the machine and it can change by a hundredfold.

What Arun wrote in the notebook is different in kind. He recorded, for each program, **the factor
by which the time is multiplied when the input doubles**, and that factor did not depend on which
machine it was measured on. One program multiplied by two, on both a fast machine and a slow one.
The other multiplied by four, on both.

That factor is a property of the *procedure*, and the underlying thing it exposes has a name: the
**growth rate** of the running time, also called its **rate of growth** or, informally and usefully,
its **shape**. Formally we treat the cost of a procedure as a function of input size — write it
`T(n)`, the number of model operations from `CPX-02` performed on an input of size `n` — and we
compare procedures by how `T(n)` grows, not by what any particular clock says.

Why is that legitimate rather than a dodge? Because a different machine changes `T(n)` by roughly a
constant factor. A machine that is thirty times faster runs *every* step thirty times faster, so it
turns `T(n)` into `T(n)/30`. Dividing by thirty does not turn a doubling into a quadrupling. The
growth rate is exactly the part of the cost that survives multiplication by a constant, which is
another way of saying it is exactly the part that survives changing machines.

There is a price, and this section will spend the next two documents on it. Throwing away the
constant throws away real information: a thirtyfold constant is a whole rack of servers, and
[1.3](1.3-what-dropping-constants-destroys.md) is about what you have agreed to stop seeing. And
"grows faster" is a statement about large inputs, which says nothing about the input you actually
have — [1.2](1.2-eventual-dominance.md) is about that.

> **Formally:** the cost of a procedure is a function `T : ℕ → ℕ` from input size to model
> operations. Two procedures are compared by the asymptotic growth of their cost functions rather
> than by measured time, because measured time is `T(n)` multiplied by an unknown, machine-specific
> constant — and growth rate is precisely the property of a function that is invariant under
> multiplication by a constant.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **PostgreSQL's query planner costs plans in units that are not seconds.** Before running your
  query the planner enumerates plans and prices each one using parameters like `seq_page_cost`
  (default 1.0), `random_page_cost` (default 4.0), `cpu_tuple_cost` (0.01) and `cpu_operator_cost`
  (0.0025) — abstract units, deliberately, because the planner has no idea what your disks do. It
  compares *shapes*: a sequential scan costs pages times a constant, a nested loop join costs outer
  rows times the inner scan, a hash join costs a build pass plus a probe pass. When people say
  "the planner chose the wrong index", the fix is nearly always to correct a *cardinality estimate*
  — how many rows the plan expects, which is the `n` — rather than to argue about milliseconds.
  Getting `n` wrong is Day 1's `CPX-02` failure, and it is the single most common cause of a
  database plan collapsing at three in the morning when a table has grown.

- **SPEC CPU, and why the industry reports ratios.** The SPEC benchmarks — the numbers processor
  vendors actually argue about — do not publish seconds as the headline result. They publish a
  *ratio* against a fixed reference machine, per workload, and then a geometric mean of those
  ratios. The entire apparatus exists because a raw time is uncomparable across compilers, memory
  configurations and years, while a ratio against a common baseline is at least arguable. Note what
  that means: the most money-soaked performance measurement in computing is a *relative* number, for
  exactly the reason Arun stopped writing down seconds.

- **Asked as:** *"How fast is your solution?"* The expected answer is a shape and an argument, not a
  time. A candidate who says "about two hundred milliseconds on my laptop" has answered a question
  about their laptop. The follow-up, always, is "and if the input were a thousand times bigger?" —
  which is the only version of the question that has a portable answer.

## 5. The mechanism

The instrument is the **doubling table**: run the procedure at sizes `n, 2n, 4n, 8n …`, record the
time, and divide each row's time by the previous row's. That ratio column is the measurement.

```
    a procedure whose work is proportional to n        a procedure whose work is proportional to n squared

         n     seconds   ratio                              n     seconds   ratio
     -----------------------------                      -----------------------------
      1000    0.000023      -                            1000    0.006819      -
      2000    0.000044   1.94                            2000    0.029205   4.28
      4000    0.000108   2.48                            4000    0.115101   3.94
      8000    0.000176   1.63                            8000    0.468638   4.07
     16000    0.000366   2.08                           16000    1.902583   4.06
     32000    0.001101   3.01                           32000    9.465736   4.98

              ^ noisy, but nowhere near 4                        ^ never near 2
```

*What to notice: the two seconds columns share no digits — the right-hand procedure is three orders
of magnitude slower at every size — and you do not need the seconds at all. The ratio columns are
what tell the two procedures apart, and they would still say 2 and 4 on a machine a hundred times
faster, because a hundredfold speed-up divides both the numerator and the denominator of every
ratio by a hundred.*

**The invariant:** *multiplying every measurement by any positive constant leaves the ratio column
unchanged.* That is the sentence that makes this instrument work, and it is worth checking on
paper: if a machine change turns each `tᵢ` into `k·tᵢ`, then each ratio `k·tᵢ₊₁ / k·tᵢ` is
`tᵢ₊₁ / tᵢ`, the same number. Anything you can read off the ratio column is machine-independent by
construction. Anything you cannot — how many milliseconds, whether it fits in a request budget — is
not.

### A trace, on the numbers Arun wrote down

| Load | Vendor A (s) | ratio | Vendor B (s) | ratio |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 500 | 1.5 | — | 11 | — |
| 1 000 | 6 | 4.0 | 22 | 2.0 |
| 2 000 | 24 | 4.0 | 45 | 2.05 |
| 4 000 | 101 | 4.2 | 89 | 1.98 |

At 4 000 properties they have crossed; at 500 they were seven times apart in the other direction.
Ten more doublings takes A's time up by `4¹⁰ ≈ 10⁶` and B's by `2¹⁰ ≈ 10³`. Starting from a hundred
seconds, that is A at roughly a hundred million seconds — years — and B at about a hundred thousand
— a day. Neither vendor ran that input. The ratio column made the claim anyway, and that is the
whole reason to prefer shapes to seconds.

## 6. Line by line

The harness that produces the table above, written from scratch, because you will use one every day
of this curriculum.

```python
import time
from collections.abc import Callable

def timed_once(procedure: Callable[[list[int]], object], data: list[int]) -> float:
    start = time.perf_counter()
    procedure(data)
    return time.perf_counter() - start
```

`time.perf_counter` rather than `time.time`. `time.time` is a wall clock: it is allowed to jump
backwards when NTP corrects it, and its resolution on some platforms is milliseconds. `perf_counter`
is monotonic and counts in the finest tick the platform can offer. A benchmark that reports a
negative duration once a month is a benchmark that used the wrong clock, and nobody ever finds that
bug, because who reruns a benchmark that only lies occasionally.

Note also what is *outside* the timed region: `data` is built by the caller. Timing your own test
data generation is the near-miss at the end of this section, and it is the most common way a
benchmark ends up measuring the benchmark.

```python
def best_of(procedure, data: list[int], repeats: int = 3) -> float:
    return min(timed_once(procedure, data) for _ in range(repeats))
```

The **minimum**, not the mean. The distribution of timings is not symmetric: the true cost is a
floor, and everything that happens to a run — a scheduler preemption, a garbage collection, another
process waking up — can only push it *upwards*. The mean averages in the interference; the minimum
is the closest you get to the run where nothing else happened. The mean is the right statistic when
you are asking "what will users experience", and the minimum is the right one when you are asking
"what does this code cost", which is today's question.

```python
def doubling_table(procedure, build: Callable[[int], list[int]],
                   start: int = 1_000, rows: int = 6) -> None:
    previous = None
    n = start
    for _ in range(rows):
        data = build(n)
        seconds = best_of(procedure, data)
        ratio = f"{seconds / previous:8.2f}" if previous else "       -"
        print(f"{n:>8} {seconds:>12.6f} {ratio}")
        previous, n = seconds, n * 2
```

`build` is a parameter and not a hardcoded `list(range(n))`, because the shape of a procedure can
depend on its input's shape as well as its size — a sorted list, a list of all-equal elements and a
random list will produce three different ratio columns for the same sorting code, and a harness that
can only generate one of them will confidently report the wrong one. That is a `CPX-02` point about
what `n` is, arriving as an API decision.

`start` is a thousand and not one, because of what §8 shows.

**The near-miss.** Here is the version that looks tidier — it builds the data where it is used:

```python
def doubling_table_wrong(procedure, build, start=1_000, rows=6) -> None:
    previous = None
    n = start
    for _ in range(rows):
        start_time = time.perf_counter()
        procedure(build(n))          # <- build is inside the timed region
        seconds = time.perf_counter() - start_time
        ...
```

Now time the halving loop from Day 2, `halvings_until_one`, using `build = lambda n: list(range(n))`
as the harness's idea of "an input of size n". Building the list is proportional to `n`. The
procedure itself does about `log₂ n` work. So the timed region is dominated, completely, by the
generator, and the ratio column reads a steady **2.00** — a beautiful, convincing, entirely false
report that a logarithmic procedure is linear. The input that kills this harness is not an unusual
one; it is *any procedure that is faster than its own test-data generator*, which is most of the
interesting ones. Whenever a ratio column says exactly what the setup code would say, suspect the
harness before the algorithm.

## 7. The cost, derived

The procedure being measured has whatever cost it has. What is worth deriving here is the cost of
**the measurement itself**, because that is what decides how large a table you can afford.

**Technique: summation.**

Let the procedure cost `c·n` for a linear one, let the table have `k` rows starting at `n₀`, and let
each row be repeated `r` times. Row `i` uses size `2ⁱ·n₀`, so the total measured work is

```
T = Σ (i = 0 … k-1) r · c · 2ⁱ · n₀
  = r · c · n₀ · (2ᵏ - 1)
  ≈ 2 · r · c · n₀ · 2^(k-1)
  = 2 × (the cost of the last row)
```

using the geometric sum `Σ 2ⁱ = 2ᵏ - 1`. So **a doubling table costs about twice its largest row**,
and every row before the last one is free in the only sense that matters. That is a small, sharp
fact with an immediate consequence: there is never a reason to skip the small rows to save time,
and there is every reason to choose the largest row carefully, because it *is* the budget. A
quadratic procedure gets the same treatment with `Σ 4ⁱ = (4ᵏ - 1)/3`, so the table costs about a
third more than its last row — even more dominated by the end.

| Case | Cost of the table | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | ≈ 2× the last row | Any linear procedure |
| Average | ≈ 1.33× the last row | Any quadratic procedure |
| Worst | ≈ 1× the last row | Anything growing faster than quadratic: the last row *is* the table |

**Space:** whatever the largest input costs to hold — `Θ(2^(k-1) · n₀)` — and note that this, not
time, is what usually stops a doubling table. The row you cannot run is often the row that does not
fit.

**What the constant hides.** Three things, all of which show up in §8. The timer has a resolution
(on this machine, `time.perf_counter` resolves to about 100 nanoseconds), so rows whose true time is
near that resolution measure noise. The first run of anything pays for cold caches and, in CPython,
for creating the objects it touches — which is why `best_of` exists at all. And the garbage
collector runs on its own schedule, so a run that happens to include a collection can be tens of
percent slower for reasons that have nothing to do with the code under test.

## 8. When it breaks

Here is the same linear procedure as in §5, timed once per row, at sizes small enough that the
measurement is not measuring anything:

```
the same linear pass, timed once each, at sizes too small to measure
     n      seconds    ratio
     1  0.000003300        -
     2  0.000001200     0.36
     4  0.000000600     0.50
     8  0.000000600     1.00
    16  0.000000700     1.17
    32  0.000001400     2.00
    64  0.000002200     1.57
   128  0.000004300     1.95
```

A ratio of **0.36** says that doubling the input made the program more than twice as fast. A ratio
of **0.50** says it again. Nothing in the code got faster; the first row paid for a cold code path
and for whatever the interpreter had to set up, and the next few rows are down in the timer's
resolution, where the numbers being divided are noise divided by noise.

This is the most common way a benchmark lies, and note the shape of the lie: it is not a wrong
number, it is a *plausible* one. Read the last three rows alone — 2.00, 1.57, 1.95 — and you would
report "linear, confirmed". Read the first three and you would report something impossible. Both
readings come from the same run.

The second failure is in the linear column of §5, and it is subtler because those sizes are not
absurd:

```
      1000    0.000023      -
      2000    0.000044   1.94
      4000    0.000108   2.48
      8000    0.000176   1.63
     16000    0.000366   2.08
     32000    0.001101   3.01
```

Ratios of 1.63 and 3.01 for a procedure that is provably linear. At twenty-three microseconds a
row, a single scheduler hiccup is a large fraction of the measurement. The procedure is fine; the
instrument is being used below its useful range.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is bigger `n`. The fix is a rule you apply
for the rest of the curriculum: **make each row take long enough that the noise is a small fraction
of it, and read the ratio column rather than any single number.** In practice that means growing the
starting size until the smallest row is a few tens of milliseconds, taking the minimum of several
repeats, and — most importantly — *distrusting a ratio column that is not close to a recognisable
number*. The recognisable numbers are few: 1 for constant, slightly above 1 for logarithmic, 2 for
linear, slightly above 2 for linearithmic, 4 for quadratic, 8 for cubic. Day 4 makes that table the
subject. A column reading 2.48 is not telling you the procedure is `n^1.3`; it is telling you to
run it again with more repeats and bigger inputs.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** The gap between "measured on my machine" and "true of this program" is where a large
share of production performance work actually lives.

*The cloud makes seconds worse, not better.* A shared virtual machine has noisy neighbours,
CPU steal, and thermal throttling that changes the clock speed while you are measuring. A CI runner
is the worst offender in most companies' toolchains: the same benchmark on the same commit can vary
by tens of percent between runs, which means an absolute-time regression gate produces false alarms
until people learn to ignore it, at which point it produces nothing at all. Teams that make this
work compare *ratios* — this commit against the previous commit, measured in the same job, on the
same runner, interleaved — for exactly the reason the ratio column is machine-independent.

*A shape change and a constant change need different responses, and confusing them wastes quarters.*
If the ratio column moved from 2 to 4, something in the code became quadratic — usually a lookup
that used to be a hash and is now a scan, or a call added inside a loop. That is a code review
question and there is a specific line to find. If the ratio column is unchanged and every row is
forty percent slower, nothing about the algorithm changed: it is a version upgrade, a serialisation
format, a compiler flag, a new allocation per element. Those are the two conversations, and the
ratio column is the instrument that tells you which one you are in.

*At `n = 10⁹` the small rows are all you will ever have.* Nobody can run the real input during
development. The extrapolation from a table at `n = 10⁴` to a claim at `n = 10⁹` is precisely the
extrapolation Arun made across ten doublings, and it is only sound if the shape does not change on
the way — which it can, when the working set leaves cache, when the data stops fitting in RAM, when
the array needs a bigger index type. Day 1's 2.3 named those leaks; the discipline here is to say
which of them your extrapolation is assuming away.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "I don't want the number, I want the column. This says the new version takes 212 ms and the old
> one took 190 ms — on what, once? Run both at four sizes, interleaved, minimum of five, and show me
> the ratios side by side. If the ratios match and everything's 10% slower, that's a constant and I
> don't care today. If the ratio went from 2 to 4 you've added a scan inside a loop and I want to
> know where before this merges."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you give the complexity and it is right:

1. *"Your laptop does this in 200 ms. What will our production box do?"* — checking whether you
   understand that you cannot answer, and that the honest reply is a shape plus a request for their
   size, not a number scaled by a guess.
2. *"You benchmarked two implementations and A is faster at every size you tried. Is A faster?"* —
   checking for the crossover instinct: faster on the sizes tried, which is a claim about the sizes
   tried. What are the ratio columns doing? This is [1.2](1.2-eventual-dominance.md) and they are
   asking whether you will overclaim.
3. *"Why is the minimum of several runs a better estimate than the mean?"* — checking whether you
   have ever actually benchmarked anything. Interference is one-sided; the floor is the signal.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Why did Arun stop writing down seconds, in one sentence, without using the words "asymptotic" or
   "complexity"?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because the seconds were facts about the two machines, and the factor by which the time grew when
   the load doubled was a fact about the two programs — and he was buying a program.
   </details>

2. A colleague reports that their optimisation made a function 40% faster at every input size they
   tried. What kind of change was it, and what would a shape change have looked like instead?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   A constant-factor change: every row improved by the same proportion, so the ratio column did not
   move. A shape change looks completely different — the ratio column itself changes, from 4 to 2
   for instance, and the improvement is small at small sizes and unbounded at large ones. Both are
   worth having; only one of them changes what the program can be used for.
   </details>

3. Your ratio column reads 2.00 exactly for a procedure you believe is logarithmic. What do you
   suspect first?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The harness, not the procedure — specifically that input generation is inside the timed region.
   Building a list of `n` elements is linear, a logarithmic procedure is much faster than that, so
   the measurement is dominated by the setup and reports the setup's shape. This is the near-miss in
   §6. Second suspicion: the procedure is being called on data whose *construction* it triggers
   lazily.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** You have a doubling table with a beautiful ratio column reading 2.00 at every row,
   from `n = 1000` to `n = 32000`, for a procedure you wrote. Construct a procedure for which that
   table is produced and which is nevertheless quadratic.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Anything whose quadratic term has a small enough coefficient not to dominate before `n = 32000`.
   `T(n) = 1000n + n²/100` is quadratic, and at `n = 32000` the linear term is 32 million against
   the quadratic term's 10 million — still winning, so the ratios read close to 2. Push the table two
   more rows and it turns over. This is why "the measurement says linear" is a claim about the range
   measured, and why the derivation in §7 of any document is the primary evidence and the benchmark
   is the check on it. [1.2](1.2-eventual-dominance.md) is this question in general form.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** The ratio column is invariant under multiplying every time by a
   constant. Name a change of machine that is *not* a constant factor, and say what it does to the
   column.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Any change that alters the cost of a single operation in a size-dependent way. A machine with
   less cache does not slow every operation down equally: it slows down the ones that miss, so a
   procedure that fits in cache at small `n` and not at large `n` gets a *shape* change in its
   measurement, not a constant. Same for a machine with less RAM once swapping starts, and for
   arbitrary-precision integers when the values grow with `n`. Day 1's 2.3 called these the leaks in
   the RAM model, and they are exactly the cases where the invariant in §5 stops holding.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-03`.** You benchmark a loop and the ratio column reads 2.00 for five rows and
   then the sixth row never finishes. What do you check, and what does the check have to do with
   Day 2?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Whether the loop terminates at all on that input — the variant. A ratio column cannot warn you
   about non-termination, because non-termination produces no row: the symptom is a benchmark that
   hangs, which people reliably misread as "the machine is slow" or "the input is too big". Before
   assuming the sixth row is merely expensive, work out how many iterations it should take, and
   compare it with how long the fifth row took. If the predicted time is a minute and it has been
   running for an hour, that is a `CPX-03` bug and no amount of waiting fixes it.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A pull request adds a performance test that fails the build if a function takes
   more than 50 milliseconds. What do you say?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   That it will fail on a loaded CI runner and pass on a fast one, so within a month it will either
   be muted or have its threshold raised until it means nothing. An absolute-time gate encodes the
   machine into the test. What survives is a relative test — this commit against the base commit,
   measured in the same job, interleaved, minimum of several — or an *operation-count* assertion,
   which has no machine in it at all: count the comparisons, count the allocations, count the
   queries, and assert on the count. Day 1's comparison counter was that idea in its first form.
   </details>
