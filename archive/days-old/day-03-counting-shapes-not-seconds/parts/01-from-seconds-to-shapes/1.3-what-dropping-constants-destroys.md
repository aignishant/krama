# 1.3 — What dropping constants destroys

## 1. The one-line answer

Throwing away the constant factor is what makes a cost claim survive a change of machine, and it
throws away the exact number that decides how many machines you have to buy.

---

## 2. The story

The casualty ward at the district hospital in Bilaspur has a whiteboard by the door, and on the
whiteboard are three columns: RED, YELLOW, GREEN.

There has been a collision on the highway — a bus and a truck, at the bend past the toll — and
Sister Mary Kurian has been standing at that door for forty minutes putting people into columns.
Not treating them. Putting them into columns. A junior doctor said something about that in his
first week, and she told him what she is telling everyone tonight: there are two of us and there
are thirty-one of them, and the only thing that matters in the first hour is the order.

So it is three columns, and the rules are crude on purpose. Can they walk? Are they breathing? Is
the bleeding controlled? That is nearly all of it. A man with a broken forearm and a woman with
four broken ribs go into the same column, and they are not remotely the same patient. Everybody in
the room knows they are not the same patient. But they are the same *decision* — both wait — and
tonight there is only capacity to make decisions.

What the board is for is order, and for order it is exactly right. Nobody in GREEN will die while
someone in RED is waiting, which is the only property that has to hold.

What the board is not for is treatment. At two in the morning, when the surgical team has come in
and the pressure has come off, the column tells you nothing you need. Which rib, which lung, how
much blood, how long since. Sister Mary has seen a houseman try to work from the board — treat the
column instead of the patient — and it is the specific kind of mistake that only happens to
somebody clever enough to trust a system.

Both of those are true at once, and the ward runs on holding them together: **the board is the
right instrument for who goes next, and the wrong instrument for what to do when they get there.**
The information that was thrown away to make three columns was not noise. It was the whole of the
patient. It was thrown away deliberately, because a nurse at a door with thirty-one people cannot
carry the whole of thirty-one patients, and a decision made from three columns in ten seconds beats
a perfect assessment that arrives at dawn.

By four in the morning the board has been wiped. It has done its job, and nothing that happens for
the rest of the night can be decided by looking at it.

---

## 3. The idea in plain language

Dropping the constant is triage.

When you say a procedure is `Θ(n)`, you have put it in a column. Everything in that column shares
one property — double the input, double the work — and that property is the one that survives a
change of machine, because a faster machine divides every cost by the same number and cannot move
anything between columns. This is the sorting done at
[1.1](1.1-seconds-do-not-travel.md)'s door, and it is right for the decision it is made for:
*which of these procedures will still be usable when the data is a thousand times bigger.*

Two things get thrown away at that door, and they are different from each other.

**The constant factor.** `0.007 · n` and `0.77 · n` are the same column. A hundredfold difference
in what the work costs — in servers, in battery, in whether the request fits in its budget — is
invisible from inside the class, and §8 measures four spellings of one linear loop that differ by a
factor of eight with no change of column at all.

**The lower-order terms.** `3n² + 500n + 20000` is in the same column as `n²`, because for large
enough `n` the first term swamps the others. At `n = 10` the quadratic term is one percent of the
cost and the "negligible" constant term is most of it. "For large enough `n`" is doing the same work
here that *eventually* did in [1.2](1.2-eventual-dominance.md), and it is the same warning.

The two abstractions have a name: procedures are grouped into **growth classes**, and the class is
what remains of the cost function after you allow yourself to multiply it by any positive constant
and to ignore anything that grows more slowly than its largest term.

The discipline is to know which decision you are making. *Will this still work at a thousand times
the size?* — read the column. *Can we serve this in eighty milliseconds on the machines we have?* —
the column cannot answer that, and no amount of asymptotic reasoning will make it. Treat the column
instead of the patient, and you get code that is provably scalable and observably too slow, which is
one of the more embarrassing places to end up because every individual claim you made was true.

> **Formally:** the growth class of `T` is what is invariant under (i) multiplication by any positive
> constant and (ii) addition or removal of any function that `T`'s dominant term eventually
> dominates. Everything the class asserts survives a change of machine; everything a change of
> machine can alter is, by construction, outside the class.

## 4. Where this actually shows up

- **The Linux scheduler, which went from `O(1)` to `O(log n)` on purpose.** From 2003 the kernel
  shipped Ingo Molnár's "O(1) scheduler", named for the property that picking the next task took
  constant time regardless of how many runnable tasks there were. In 2007 it was replaced by the
  Completely Fair Scheduler, which keeps runnable tasks in a red-black tree and picks in `O(log n)`.
  The class got *worse* and the kernel took it, because the O(1) scheduler's constant-time pick came
  bundled with a pile of interactivity heuristics that behaved badly for desktop workloads, and CFS
  produced better latency behaviour with a simpler rule. With `n` in the dozens, `log n` is about
  five, and five times a small constant is nothing next to getting the *policy* right. A famous
  algorithm-class label lost to the things the label does not capture.

- **Capacity planning, which is nothing but constants.** Nobody provisions servers from a complexity
  class. The arithmetic that decides a cluster's size is requests per second times milliseconds per
  request divided by cores, and every term in it is a constant that asymptotic analysis discarded.
  This is why performance work in production so often consists of shaving a constant — batching
  writes, hoisting an allocation out of a loop, replacing a per-row round trip with one query — with
  no change to any complexity anywhere. A 40% constant improvement on a fleet of a thousand machines
  is four hundred machines, and Phase 33's capacity-estimation days are entirely about arithmetic of
  this kind.

- **Asked as:** *"Your solution is `O(n)` and mine is `O(n log n)`, so why is mine faster on the
  test data?"* The question is a trap only for people who think the class is the answer. The reply
  that gets you the offer names the constant: what does one step cost in each, where does the time
  actually go, and at what size does the ordering flip — which is [1.2](1.2-eventual-dominance.md)
  and this document, in one breath.

## 5. The mechanism

Take a single cost function and watch where its cost actually lives as `n` grows.

```
    T(n) = 3n² + 500n + 20000        share of the total contributed by each term

        n         3n²          500n       20000         3n²     500n    20000
    -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
       10         300         5,000      20,000        1.2%    19.8%    79.0%
      100      30,000        50,000      20,000       30.0%    50.0%    20.0%
     1000   3,000,000       500,000      20,000       85.2%    14.2%     0.6%
    10000 300,000,000     5,000,000      20,000       98.4%     1.6%     0.0%
   100000  3.0 × 10¹⁰     5.0 × 10⁷      20,000       99.8%     0.2%     0.0%

              ^ the term the class keeps      ^ the terms the class throws away
```

*What to notice: at `n = 10` the term that names the class contributes about one percent of the
cost, and the term the class discards contributes seventy-nine. The class is not wrong at `n = 10`
— it is silent there. It becomes informative somewhere around a thousand and is essentially the
whole truth by ten thousand.*

**The invariant:** *multiplying a cost function by any positive constant, or adding any term its
dominant term eventually dominates, leaves the growth class unchanged.* Read that as a statement
about what the class can and cannot see: every question whose answer changes under those two
operations is a question the class cannot answer. "How many servers" changes when you multiply by a
constant. "Will it still work at a thousand times the size" does not.

### A trace, in the other direction

Four spellings of the same linear sum over a million integers, all `Θ(n)`, measured on this
machine, minimum of five runs:

| Spelling | ms | × fastest |
|---|---:|---:|
| `sum(values)` | 6.53 | 1.0× |
| `for v in values: total += v` | 26.17 | 4.1× |
| `for i in range(len(values)): total += values[i]` | 39.39 | 6.1× |
| `for v in values: total = add(total, v)` | 51.36 | 8.0× |

One column on the triage board. Eight times apart at the door. Every one of these is the correct
answer to "what is the complexity", and if the budget is ten milliseconds, three of them are
unshippable and the class does not distinguish them.

## 6. Line by line

The instrument this section deserves is not an algorithm — it is the arithmetic that turns a growth
class back into a number, which is the step people skip.

```python
def estimate_seconds(cost_terms: dict[str, float], n: int, ns_per_op: float) -> float:
    """cost_terms maps a term's shape to its coefficient: {"n2": 3, "n": 500, "1": 20000}."""
    operations = (
        cost_terms.get("n2", 0) * n * n
        + cost_terms.get("n", 0) * n
        + cost_terms.get("1", 0)
    )
    return operations * ns_per_op / 1e9
```

The parameter that matters is `ns_per_op`, and it is a parameter rather than a literal because it is
a property of the machine and the *kind* of operation, not of the algorithm. Measuring it is a
five-line experiment, and the answer on CPython is nothing like the answer people assume: from §5's
table, a Python-level loop iteration doing one addition costs about 26 nanoseconds, and the same
work inside `sum()` costs about 6.5.

```python
    return operations * ns_per_op / 1e9
```

Note what this line quietly asserts: that every operation costs the same. That is the RAM model of
`CPX-02` again, and it is exactly as false here as it was on Day 1 — a cache miss is not an L1 hit,
an `int` addition on 2⁶⁴ is not one on 2⁶⁴⁰⁰⁰. The estimate is worth making anyway, because being
wrong by three times is enormously better than not knowing whether the answer is milliseconds or
days, and because the act of writing down `ns_per_op` forces you to have measured something.

```python
def fits_in_budget(cost_terms: dict[str, float], n: int,
                   ns_per_op: float, budget_ms: float) -> bool:
    return estimate_seconds(cost_terms, n, ns_per_op) * 1000 <= budget_ms
```

This is the function that the complexity class cannot be substituted for. `Θ(n)` has no opinion on
whether the work fits in eighty milliseconds; `estimate_seconds` does, and its answer is only as
good as the constant you fed it. Writing this down as code rather than doing it in your head has a
side effect worth having: the coefficients become visible, arguable, and revisable in review.

**The near-miss.** Here is the estimate everyone makes in their head, and it is off by two orders of
magnitude:

```python
# "it's linear, n is a million, so that's a million operations -- about a millisecond"
estimated_ms = n / 1_000_000
```

The hidden assumption is `ns_per_op = 1`, which is roughly right for a tight loop in C on modern
hardware and roughly a *fortieth* of the truth for a Python-level loop. The input that kills it is
the ordinary one: `n = 1_000_000` in the second row of §5's table takes **26 milliseconds**, not
one. Plan a request budget with this estimate and you will ship something twenty-six times over
budget while being able to prove that its complexity is optimal.

The deeper problem is that the near-miss does not look like an estimate at all. It looks like the
complexity class being used directly, which is the mistake the whole document is about: a class is a
column, and a column has no units.

## 7. The cost, derived

**Technique: summation** — and this time the interesting part is not the shape, which is obvious,
but the constant, which is what the section is about.

All four spellings in §5 iterate once per element and perform one addition, so each is

```
T(n) = Σ (i = 1 … n) c  =  c · n
```

and every one of them is `Θ(n)`. The difference lives entirely in `c`, so itemise it. From the
measured table, dividing by 10⁶ elements:

```
sum(values)                              6.53 ms / 10⁶  =  6.5 ns per element
for v in values                         26.17 ms / 10⁶  = 26.2 ns
for i in range(len(values))             39.39 ms / 10⁶  = 39.4 ns
for v in values, through a function call 51.36 ms / 10⁶ = 51.4 ns
```

Now account for the differences, because each one is a nameable cost:

- `sum()` runs its loop in C: no bytecode dispatch per element, but still one Python `int` object per
  addition. **6.5 ns** is roughly the cost of that object arithmetic alone.
- Adding a Python-level loop costs about **20 ns** per element on top: fetching the next item,
  dispatching a handful of bytecodes, rebinding a local.
- Indexing instead of iterating adds another **13 ns**: `range` produces an integer object, then
  `values[i]` performs a bounds check and a dereference, where the iterator version simply advanced a
  pointer.
- Wrapping the addition in a function call adds another **12 ns**: a frame is pushed and popped a
  million times.

| Case | Cost | The input that causes it |
|---|---|---|
| Best | `6.5 ns · n` | The C-level spelling, any input |
| Average | `26 ns · n` | The ordinary Python loop |
| Worst | `51 ns · n` | The spelling with a call in the loop body |

Every row is `Θ(n)`; the table is the part the class deleted. Note that "best/average/worst" here
are not about *inputs* at all — this loop is oblivious in the sense Day 2's 1.1 gave. They are about
spellings, and no complexity analysis in the world distinguishes them.

**Space:** `Θ(1)` auxiliary for all four. The class is silent about the constant here too — the
function-call version touches a new frame per element, which is a real allocation the others do not
make.

**What the constant hides.** In this section, precisely: interpreter dispatch, object allocation,
bounds checks, and frame setup. Each is a fixed number of nanoseconds, none of them appears in any
complexity claim, and together they are the difference between shipping and not.

## 8. When it breaks

The measured output, run again so you can see there is no trick in it:

```
                      spelling        ms   x fastest
                   sum(values)      6.53        1.0x
               for v in values     26.17        4.1x
   for i in range(len(values))     39.39        6.1x
   for v in values, via a call     51.36        8.0x
```

Four `Θ(n)` procedures, computing the same result from the same data on the same machine, spread
over a factor of eight. If the budget is 10 ms, the first is fine and the rest are failures, and
there is no complexity argument anywhere that can tell them apart — every one of them is optimal by
the only measure this document's class provides.

The failure at the other end of the size range comes from the table in §5:

```
   n = 10:   3n² = 300   500n = 5,000   20000 = 20,000     total = 25,300
             the term that names the class is 1.2% of the cost
```

Call this procedure "quadratic" while your `n` is ten, plan around that, and every decision you make
will be about a term contributing one percent of the runtime. The usual form of the mistake is
optimising the wrong term: someone spends a week improving the `3n²` term to `n²` and the program
gets 0.8% faster, because at their size the cost was almost entirely the constant `20000` — a
per-call setup that a five-minute change could have removed.

**The fix, and why it is not just a patch.** The patch is to measure before optimising, which is
true and which everybody already says. The fix is a habit with two halves, and the second half is
the one people skip: **carry the constant alongside the class, and say which of the two your current
decision depends on.** "This is `Θ(n)` at about 26 ns per element, so a million elements is 26 ms,
which does not fit an 80 ms budget with four other stages in it" is a sentence that decides
something. "This is `Θ(n)`" decides nothing on its own, and its confident tone is the problem — it
sounds like an answer. The whole of Phase 33's estimation work is that sentence, written out at
system scale, and it starts as a habit here.

## 9. In production

**At scale.** The constant is the part that costs money, and three specific things follow.

*Constants set the fleet size; classes set whether a fleet is possible at all.* A service handling
30 000 requests per second at 12 ms of CPU each needs about 360 cores of headroom before you have
begun to talk about redundancy — and every one of those numbers is a constant. Halve the 12 ms and
you have deleted 180 cores; improve the complexity class of something that runs on a fixed-size
input and you have deleted nothing. The two kinds of improvement are worth completely different
amounts depending on whether `n` is growing, and knowing which is which is a large part of what
"senior" means in a performance conversation.

*Tail latency is a constant-factor story almost every time.* The p99 of a request path is usually
not a different algorithm — it is a cache miss, a garbage collection, a lock wait, a retry, a cold
connection. None of those appear in any complexity class, all of them show up in the graph the
on-call engineer is looking at, and the fix is nearly always to remove a fixed cost from a path
rather than to remove a factor of `n`.

*Class improvements are permanent; constant improvements are borrowed.* This is the argument for the
other side, and it is the reason the abstraction earns its keep. A 40% constant win can be given back
by the next runtime upgrade, a new serialisation format, or a machine with different cache
behaviour. A change from `Θ(n²)` to `Θ(n log n)` cannot be given back by anything, and it keeps
paying as the data grows — which is why, when you have to choose, the class is the better
investment, and why the triage board is at the door and not in the operating theatre.

**What a senior reviewer says.**

> "'It's O(n), so it's fine' — fine at what size, and at how many nanoseconds an element? We're
> calling this per request with n around fifty thousand. If that's the loop I think it is, that's
> more than a millisecond of pure interpreter overhead before it has done anything useful, and we
> have an eighty-millisecond budget with five other stages in it. Give me the number, not the
> letter."

**What an interviewer probes.** After you give the correct complexity:

1. *"Roughly how long does that take on a million elements, in Python?"* — checking whether you have
   ever measured anything. An answer within a factor of ten is a strong signal; "I'd have to
   benchmark it" is acceptable followed by an estimate, and is a weak answer on its own.
2. *"You have two `O(n)` implementations. How do you choose?"* — checking whether you can talk about
   constants concretely: allocations per element, whether the inner loop is in C, cache behaviour,
   and what the input distribution is.
3. *"When is a constant-factor improvement worth more than a complexity improvement?"* — checking
   judgement. When `n` is bounded by something real and the constant is on a hot path — a fixed-size
   header, a bounded batch, a page of results — the constant is the entire game.

## 10. Check yourself

Do these the day *after* you read this, with the document closed.

1. Name the two things a growth class discards, and give an example where each one is the whole cost.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The constant factor and the lower-order terms. The constant is the whole cost in §5's four
   spellings — same class, eight times apart. The lower-order terms are the whole cost in §5's first
   row, where `3n² + 500n + 20000` at `n = 10` is ninety-nine percent terms the class ignores.
   </details>

2. Why is dropping the constant *legitimate* rather than merely convenient?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Because a change of machine multiplies every operation's cost by roughly the same factor, so the
   constant is precisely the machine-dependent part. What remains after you allow multiplication by
   any constant is what will still be true on hardware nobody has built yet — that is the whole
   argument of [1.1](1.1-seconds-do-not-travel.md), and it is why the class travels and the seconds
   do not.
   </details>

3. A colleague improves a routine from `3n²` to `n²` operations. Under what circumstances is that
   worth nothing at all?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   When `n` is small enough that the quadratic term is not where the time goes. At `n = 10` in §5's
   function, the `3n²` term is 1.2% of the cost, so cutting it by two thirds saves under one percent.
   It is also worth nothing when the routine is not on a path anyone waits for. Both of those are
   questions about the constant and the workload, not about the class, and neither can be answered
   from the complexity.
   </details>

4. **Break it.** You are told procedure A is `Θ(n)` and procedure B is `Θ(n log n)`, and that both
   are correct implementations of the same specification. Construct a situation in which choosing A
   is a firing offence.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Make A's constant enormous and the input bounded: A is `Θ(n)` with 50 µs per element because it
   makes a network call per element, B is `Θ(n log n)` entirely in memory, and `n` is capped at 5000
   by a page size. A takes 250 ms and 5000 network round trips; B takes under a millisecond. Both
   complexity claims are true, and A is unshippable. The general recipe is: put the constant in a
   different *kind* of operation — a syscall, a disk seek, a network hop — because the RAM model
   charges those the same as an addition, and reality charges them a million times more. That is the
   Day 1 leak (`CPX-02`) and this document's blind spot arriving together.
   </details>

5. **Connects to `CPX-02`.** §6's `estimate_seconds` multiplies an operation count by a single
   `ns_per_op`. Name the Day 1 assumption that licenses that multiplication, and the two situations
   where it is most badly wrong.
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The uniform-cost assumption of the RAM model: every operation is charged one unit, so total time
   is operations times a fixed per-operation cost. It is most badly wrong (i) when memory access
   patterns differ — a sequential scan and a pointer chase have identical operation counts and can
   differ by two orders of magnitude, which is Day 1's cache leak — and (ii) when the values grow
   with `n`, so arbitrary-precision arithmetic makes a single "operation" cost more as the input
   gets bigger, which is Day 1's big-integer leak and Day 2's halving measurement.
   </details>

6. **Connects to `CPX-03`.** You are shown a loop with an invariant and a variant, both correct, and
   asked for its cost. What does the variant give you, and what does it not give you, in the
   vocabulary of this document?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   The variant gives you the *class*: a measure that starts at `V₀` and falls by at least one bounds
   the iteration count, so it names the column. It gives you nothing about the constant, because it
   counts iterations rather than the cost of an iteration — Day 2's halving loop ran `log₂ n` times
   and cost `Θ(b²)` on real hardware, since each iteration's price grew with the operand's width. A
   termination argument and a running-time estimate are the same argument only up to a constant, and
   the constant is the part this document says you have to go and measure.
   </details>

7. **In review.** A pull request's description says "changes an `O(n²)` algorithm to `O(n)`" and the
   diff adds a dictionary keyed by a tuple built per element. What do you check before approving?
   <details><summary>answer</summary>

   Two things. First, that the class claim is true in the model *and* on this data: hashing a tuple
   is not free, and if the key construction allocates per element the constant may have grown enough
   to lose at the sizes this code actually sees — ask for the doubling table at real sizes. Second,
   the memory: `O(n²)` time with `O(1)` space has become `O(n)` time with `O(n)` space, which can be
   an excellent trade or an outage, depending on whether `n` is bounded and what else is resident.
   Class changes that trade one resource for another need the second resource's arithmetic done
   explicitly, and "it's O(n) now" hides that entirely.
   </details>
